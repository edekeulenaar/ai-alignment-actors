    The actors in AI alignment

    A working paper by Emillie de Keulenaar, Rasmus Kleis Nielsen and Diyi Liu from the Power Over Platforms project.

    Introduction

    One of the major controversies that AI has brought to societal debate has been its renewed role in public access to information (Branford, Soulier, and Fichtner 2025). One once spoke to "algorithms" as a catch-all denominator of the underlying infrastructures and politics behind information management (Bowker and Star 2000), be that as ranking systems (Rieder, Matamoros-Fernández, and Coromina 2018), newsfeeds (Kalogeropoulos et al. 2019), recommenders (Möller et al. 2018) and a variety of content moderation systems (Gillespie 2022; Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020). While that is still very much the case, there is an understanding that AI systems — LLMs, in particular — come to replace these as ever more intuitive and precise personalization systems. And as seamless as this shift may feel, the political implications remain profound: both algorithmic and AI-based information systems get to conjugate the circulation of information with societal norms.

    In this sense, another political consequence of this shift is the question of who gets to decide and operationalise societal norms into technical systems, be these epistemic (for what concerns definitions of "misinformation" and other types of information integrity); bias (be it representational or political); harm (personal or societal); and a myriad other contested concepts (Collier, Hidalgo, and Maciuceanu 2006). Part of that question is one of inclusion: who is consulted about these norms, and what is their participation in technical design? Another is a question of negotiation — or struggle, should we confront the irredeemably diverse nature of stakeholder interests: how are different actors invited to deliberate and collaborate in the design of technical values (Habe 1989)? Algorithmic systems had called for a variety of "participative", "collaborative" or "reflective" methods (Sengers et al. 2005), yielding protocols like algorithmic pluralism (Szymielewicz 2025) or "prosocial design" (Schirch 2025).

    But what does this look like in alignment systems? Who participates in alignment processes, and in what capacity? Like algorithmic systems before it, alignment is constituted by a stack of contributions from multiple actors: legislations (where applicable, or if available); data production and curation; norm definition (be those ideal AI behaviours, or risks); the training of each conduct, or mitigation of each risk; their benchmarking and evaluation; and so on. Each of these steps constitute "governance mechanisms" (Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020) in their own right: the selection of a knowledge base; the adjudication and domestication of different speech norms (de Keulenaar 2025); and the legitimation of each learned behaviour. This also means an emerging AI governance infrastructure, where AI companies crystallise norms in "principles" or "charter" documents; devise ideal uses and misuses in trust & safety policies; disclose training design in model cards; announce normative pivots and other decisions in blog posts, and so on.

    The question posed above is broad enough that we restrict this article into a mapping exercise. We explore what types of actors are involved in five alignment steps: crafting documentation, defining risks and ideal conducts, training, and benchmarking. The method builds a static visualisation from a scripted LLM-assisted extraction pipeline: 213 alignment-relevant documents, drawn from a Zotero bibliography and the latest company-policy snapshots, are resolved from local PDFs, HTML/Markdown snapshots or live URLs, then read end-to-end by Gemini against a fixed schema. Each extraction produces row-level evidence linking a document to conducts or risks, training or mitigation methods, benchmarks, evaluators, companies, models, actors and actor types, yielding details_all.csv with 2,062 rows and 43 fields. Post-processing scripts classify training methods and benchmarks into controlled taxonomies, cache those classifications for cheap reruns, validate joins against the bibliography, and aggregate the flat CSV into docs/data.json for the GitHub Pages interface. The resulting site groups documents, conducts, risks, training methods and benchmarks by company and category, colours items by primary actor type, preserves cited actors separately, and supports filtering, highlighting and cross-linking back to the underlying source passages and page references.

    Method

    The visualisation rests on a structured reading of 213 alignment documents — model cards, system cards, company policies, principles documents, grant calls, audits, blog announcements and research papers — analysed end-to-end by a large language model against a fixed schema. Each document yields one or more rows of the form (document × conduct or risk × training method × benchmark × actors). The result is a flat dataset of 2,062 rows describing 290 conducts, 918 risks, 557 training methods and 788 benchmarks across 29 companies, together with the actors named at every stage. The pipeline is fully scripted and resumable; the entire extraction can be re-run cheaply whenever the schema or taxonomy is revised.

    Corpus

    The corpus combines two parallel sources. The first is a Zotero bibliography of 181 alignment-relevant publications — model cards and system cards from the major labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, Mistral, DeepSeek, Microsoft, Alibaba, Black Forest Labs, Aleph Alpha, and others), accompanied by published principles documents, charters, audit reports and grant calls. Each item carries a Zotero key, item type, author, year, URL, and a file attachment (PDF or HTML snapshot). The second source is a folder of 53 deduplicated company policies: acceptable-use, privacy, terms-of-service, developer terms, data-processor agreements and related documents, tracked through time via dated snapshots stored as Markdown. For each (product, policy type) pair only the most recent snapshot is analysed, on the basis that operative policy is what affects training.

    Document resolution is layered. The script first looks for a local PDF attachment, then a local HTML snapshot (passed through trafilatura for clean markdown extraction so the result fits within the model's context window), then a live URL. Live URLs are fetched through a cascading chain — httpx, then requests, then a curl subprocess, then Jina Reader as a final content-extraction fallback — and wrapped in a retry loop with exponential back-off to absorb transient SSL or DNS errors.

    Document-type taxonomy

    Each document is classified into one of ten types — Model card, Company policy, Principle, Grant, Announcement, Audit, Initiative, Report, Research, or Other — with an optional sub-type for policies (Acceptable Use, Privacy, Terms of Service, Developer Terms, Data Processor Agreement, etc.). For company policies, the schema is restricted to information that informs how the model is trained, moderated, filtered or evaluated; user-facing usage rules, billing terms and account terms are explicitly excluded. This keeps the dataset focused on alignment governance and avoids drowning it in the boilerplate of consumer contracts.

    Per-document extraction

    Each document is read in a single call to Gemini (gemini-3-flash-preview) with a 3,084-token system prompt that defines the schema, the document-type taxonomy, the conduct and risk vocabularies, the training-method categories, the actor-type vocabulary, and slug-generation rules for stable cross-document identifiers. The model is instructed to return a JSON object with a single key rows, whose value is an array of row objects. Row granularity is one row per (conduct or risk × benchmark) combination found in the document. A conduct or risk with three benchmarks therefore generates three rows, in which the training/mitigation fields are repeated; a conduct or risk discussed but not benchmarked still produces one row, so it appears in the dataset.

    For each row the schema records 41 fields, grouped as follows:

    • Document classification — pub_type, policy_type, plus the Zotero-derived key, item_type, pub_year, pub_author, pub_title, pub_url.
    • Company & model — company, company_country, company_model.
    • Document contributors — pipe-separated lists of named actors and their types, including any actors cited from external publications inside the verbatim conduct/risk passages (pub_actors, pub_actors_type, pub_actor_type_id, pub_actor_page).
    • Conduct or risk — verbatim definition, canonical category from a controlled vocabulary, conduct vs risk flag, verbatim item name, source page (risk_conduct_definition, risk_conduct_category, risk_conduct_type, risk_conduct_item, pub_risk_conduct_definition_page).
    • Specific actor credited with defining the conduct or risk, if and only if the document explicitly credits one (specific_risk_conduct_actor, specific_risk_conduct_actor_category). When the document defines the concept inline without external attribution, both fields are null.
    • Training or mitigation method — verbatim description, category from the eight-class taxonomy below, page, and actors involved (risk_conduct_training_verbatim, risk_conduct_training_category, pub_training_page, risk_conduct_training_actor, risk_conduct_training_actor_type).
    • Benchmark — verbatim name, author and author type, page (risk_conduct_benchmark, risk_conduct_benchmark_author, risk_conduct_benchmark_author_type, pub_benchmark_page).
    • External evaluator — third-party evaluators (e.g. METR, ARC Evals, the UK AI Safety Institute) when commissioned (external_evaluator, external_evaluator_type, pub_evaluator_page).

    Extraction is parallelised across six worker threads and incremental: a per-document .analyse_done.txt log tracks successful extractions, so any failure is automatically retried on the next run while completed documents are skipped.

    Controlled vocabularies

    Free-form extraction is paired with two stages of controlled vocabulary. The model is constrained to choose one of nine actor types — internal, private, academic, research institute, governmental, nonprofit, public, public consultation, other — to which we add the meta-category multiple when a row contains several types. Conduct categories are drawn from a 17-class vocabulary covering norms such as Honesty, Helpfulness, Harmlessness, Anti-discrimination, Political neutrality, Factuality, Reasoning, Safety and Knowledge. Risk categories are drawn from a 40-class vocabulary covering specific risk areas — CBRN, Cybersecurity, Bias, Sycophancy, Reward hacking, Persuasion, Misalignment, Hallucinations, Child Safety, Jailbreaking, and so on. Where a document uses language that does not map, the model returns Other: <specify>; these are reviewed and either folded back into the vocabulary or kept as an open category.

    Training and mitigation methods are mapped to one of eight categories: Preference learning (RLHF, RLAIF, DPO, reward modelling), Supervised training (SFT, instruction tuning, demonstration fine-tuning, deliberative alignment), Reward shaping (adversarial reward functions, reward capping, rule-based rewards), Data curation (filtering, deduplication, synthetic data, PII redaction), Adversarial training (red-teaming data, adversarial examples), Inference-time filtering (content classifiers, safety filters, moderation APIs), Hard constraints (system-prompt rules, refusal policies, hardcoded refusals), and Runtime safeguards (trip wires, model lookahead, runtime monitoring, scratchpad oversight, sandboxing). A pipe separator is used when several categories co-apply.

    The prompt is strict about what counts as a training method. Constitutional frameworks (e.g. Constitutional AI, Anthropic's Constitution) are treated as sources of norms — listed in pub_actors or risk_conduct_definition — and not as techniques; the underlying technique is RLAIF or SFT. Narratives about how a risk arises are likewise excluded from the training fields.

    Post-extraction classification

    Two further classification passes are run after extraction, against the unique values discovered in the data. The first maps each training description to a specific technique within a structured per-category taxonomy (e.g. Preference learning → RLHF, RLAIF, DPO, KTO, Reward modelling; Supervised training → SFT, deliberative alignment, rejection-sampling fine-tuning; etc.). This pass is grounded in Ji et al.'s AI Alignment: a Comprehensive Survey (2023), passed to Gemini as taxonomic context. The second pass categorises each benchmark into one of 24 domains — Safety / Harmlessness, Refusal / Over-refusal, Honesty / Factuality, Hallucination, Bias / Fairness, Toxicity, Cybersecurity, CBRN, Privacy, Jailbreak / Adversarial, Agentic / Autonomy, capability sub-categories (coding, reasoning, maths, multilingual, multimodal, long context, instruction following), Persuasion, Sycophancy, Reward hacking, Misalignment / Scheming, Internal evaluation suite, Other.

    Both passes work on the deduplicated set of values rather than the full 2,062 rows, which keeps the API cost modest (roughly 700 unique training entries and 800 unique benchmarks). Results are cached to JSON; re-running the classifier is free for already-seen values, so the taxonomy can be revised iteratively without re-paying for stable classifications. Both classifiers retry transient network errors with capped exponential back-off (up to ten attempts at 1 s, 2 s, 4 s, 8 s, 16 s, then 30 s with jitter).

    Aggregation for the interface

    The flat CSV is reshaped into a compact JSON file (docs/data.json) consumed by the front-end. Conducts, risks and misuses are aggregated on (company × item); training methods on (company × risk/conduct category × training item); benchmarks on (company × risk/conduct category × benchmark name). The original training-method and benchmark-domain classifications are preserved as item metadata.

    For each aggregated item, the actor types appearing on its source rows are split into two sets:

    • Author types — every actor whose name matches the document's pub_author or company (loose substring match, so "Google" ↔ "Google DeepMind" still counts).
    • Cited types — every other actor named anywhere in the document's actor columns, plus any specific_risk_conduct_actor explicitly credited with defining the conduct or risk.

    Square colour is section-specific:

    • Documents, Misuses — colour = source-document author type.
    • Conducts, Risks — colour = specific_risk_conduct_actor_category if a specific actor is explicitly credited; otherwise the source-document author type.
    • Training — colour = risk_conduct_training_actor_type.
    • Benchmarking — colour = risk_conduct_benchmark_author_type.

    In every case the colour resolves to a single type when the contributing actors share one, "multiple" when more than one, or "unknown" otherwise. The cited types do not influence colour. They drive the second state of the actor-type chips: clicking a chip once highlights items where the type is the AUTHOR for that block; clicking again broadens the highlight to items where the type appears anywhere else on the row (the "+ cited" superset).

    Interface

    The site is a single static page (index.html + data.json) published via GitHub Pages. The sidebar contains:

    • A scrolling section nav with sub-navigation for Documents, Conducts, Risks, Training and Benchmark.
    • An Actors key: click an actor-type chip to highlight every element associated with that type. "Multiple" entries are treated as the union of their component types, so clicking academic highlights any item whose aggregated actor types include academic, even when its primary type is multiple.
    • Multi-select dropdown filters for year, country, company and model.
    • A view toggle for naming squares (compact view shows colour-coded blocks; named view replaces them with labelled chips that contain the verbatim item name).
    • A show-all-companies toggle that lifts the default top-ten cap.
    • A company list with document counts, where clicking a company name acts as a shortcut filter.

    The main canvas is divided into five blocks (Documents, Conducts, Risks, Training, Benchmarking). In each block, rows are categories and columns are the top-ten companies for that block. Squares are colour-coded by the primary actor type; document icons use a pub-type-specific glyph and a coloured border. Hovering a square or a document icon shows an info card with the verbatim definition, "Found in" links (with page numbers), contributors, and any external evaluator. Clicking pins the card and highlights every element linked to that square across all five blocks — sources, conducts and risks defined or mentioned, training methods and benchmarks applied. Re-clicking dismisses the selection. Categories themselves are clickable too: clicking a category label highlights every item in that category and its full set of cross-links. Categories can be sorted alphabetically, by quantity, by newest-source, or by oldest-source publication year.

    Methodological limitations

    Three limitations are worth stating up front. First, model cards and policies are governance artefacts: they record what companies choose to disclose. Their silences are themselves a finding, but it means the dataset reproduces the asymmetries of disclosure across the industry — well-documented labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) are over-represented relative to those that publish less. The visualisation's "top ten by document count" is not a ranking of alignment activity but of alignment disclosure.

    Second, LLM-assisted extraction trades exhaustive human reading for coverage. Gemini may miss content buried in footnotes, conflate training methods with risk narratives, or assign actor types inconsistently across documents that use similar phrasing for different parties. Each row preserves the verbatim source passage and a page number to facilitate manual verification, and a separate validation script checks every row's join keys against the bibliography.

    Third, the controlled vocabularies are necessarily incomplete. Vocabularies have grown iteratively as the documents are read; new categories are added where companies use language the existing taxonomy cannot capture (Multilingual incoherences, Encouraging mental illness, Historical revisionism, Reward hacking). The benchmark taxonomy in particular collapses substantively different evaluations into a single bucket where their published descriptions are too brief to disambiguate (e.g. internal company suites that share a name but differ in content from one model version to the next).

    Files & reproducibility

    The pipeline is fully scripted. The principal files are:

    • analyse_model_cards.py — main Gemini extraction driver: loads the Zotero CSV and the policy snapshots, resolves each document to a usable input (PDF / Markdown / HTML / URL with retry chain), calls Gemini against the 3,084-token system prompt, injects Zotero metadata into each returned row, and appends to details_all.csv. Resumable; parallelised over six worker threads.
    • classify_training_and_benchmarks.py — adds risk_conduct_training_item and risk_conduct_benchmark_category columns by classifying unique values against the two reference taxonomies. Caches results in .classify_*_cache.json.
    • build_site_data.py — aggregates the flat CSV into the JSON shape consumed by the front-end (documents, conducts, risks, trainings, benchmarks with cross-links and union actor-type sets).
    • validate_outputs.py — sanity-checks the join keys between the bibliography and the extraction outputs.
    • PoP - Zotero alignment database.csv — bibliography (181 records).
    • PoP - Company policies/ — dated policy snapshots (folder structure: Product (Company) / Policy type / ISO-timestamp.md).
    • details_all.csv — final flat extraction (2,062 rows × 41 columns).
    • docs/data.json — aggregated dataset consumed by the front-end.

    How to read this visualisation

    What is a stack?

    One stack = one group of actors (one actor type, or one company when you switch the Group dropdown). The four parallelograms inside it are the four alignment stages, top to bottom:

    1. Conducts — desired behaviours a model is trained for.
    2. Risks — harms the company names and tries to mitigate.
    3. Training — the techniques used (RLHF, SFT, filters, …).
    4. Benchmarks — the evaluations used to grade the model.

    What is a node?

    Each circle is one actor (a person, an organisation, a consortium) that appears in at least one document touching that stage.

    • Filled circles are actors who are explicitly credited at that stage (the named training actor, benchmark author, or the actor a conduct / risk is attributed to) or who authored the source document.
    • Hollow circles are actors merely cited in a document on that stage — referenced without doing the work themselves. Toggle them off with Include cited actors.

    Click any node to isolate the network it belongs to: the actor lights up wherever it appears across the four stages, every grey co-mention edge it touches is kept visible (its direct neighbours fade to a softer state), and the cyan vertical lines linking the actor across layers are highlighted. Everything else dims. Click the background or press Esc to clear.

    Where does a node sit on the plane?

    Position is computed by a force-directed layout running on every plane separately:

    • Each grey edge inside the plane is a spring that pulls two actors together. An edge exists when both actors are named in the same source document at this stage — co-mention is the only signal.
    • Every node also repels every other node, so circles cannot overlap.
    • A weak central pull keeps the cloud from drifting off the slab.

    The simulation runs 260 ticks and is then frozen. The resulting coordinates are projected into the plane's isometric parallelogram. Node radius scales with the actor's mention count (sqrt-mapped), so the biggest circles are the most-cited actors at that stage.

    Labels. To keep the slabs readable, only the top 10 most-connected actors per plane get a permanent label — the ones with the most co-mentions. Hover any other circle to reveal its label transiently. Tick Show all labels in the controls to label every node at once.

    What does the distance between two nodes mean?

    Distance ≈ inverse of co-mention frequency. The more documents two actors share at this stage, the stronger the springs pulling them together, and the closer they end up. A few practical readings:

    • Tight clusters = actors who keep showing up together in the same model cards, audits or grant programmes.
    • Lone dots near the edge of the plane = actors mentioned only once, or only in documents where no peer of the same type appears.
    • Two clusters far apart = two disjoint communities of practice at this stage.

    Absolute position on the plane (left vs right, near vs far edge) has no meaning — only relative distance does. Reloads can rearrange clusters; the structure of who-belongs-with-whom is what is stable.

    What about the cyan vertical edges?

    A cyan line connects the same actor appearing on two stages — i.e. it tracks an actor's enrolment through the alignment stack. Each one is split at its midpoint:

    • The half nearest the source slab is drawn behind the slab → the line disappears into it on the way out.
    • The half nearest the target slab is painted over the slab → the line emerges from it on arrival.

    Read denser cyan as: this actor type is enrolled in more than one stage of alignment.

    Caveats

    • Each plane is capped at the top-N most-mentioned actors (configurable). Capped actors prefer explicitly-credited / author roles over cited-only.
    • Force layouts are non-deterministic across reloads; cluster membership is stable, cluster position is not.
    • An actor's type is taken from the row where they appear; the same name with different types (e.g. an academic who also founds a nonprofit) can land in different stacks. This is a feature, not a bug, but worth knowing.
