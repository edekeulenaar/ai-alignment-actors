      The actors in AI alignment

      Introduction

      One of the major controversies AI has brought to societal debate concerns its renewed role in public access to information . One once spoke of "algorithms" as a catch-all denominator for the underlying infrastructures and politics behind information management , be that as ranking systems , newsfeeds , recommenders or a variety of content moderation systems . There is now an understanding that AI systems — LLMs, in particular — come to replace these as ever more intuitive and precise personalization systems. Seamless as this shift may feel, the political implications remain profound: both algorithmic and AI-based information systems condition the levers through which access to and the circulation of information are aligned with societal norms.

      A central political consequence of this shift is the question of who gets to decide and operationalise societal norms into technical systems. Perhaps accidentally, AI companies have again ended up detaining most decision-making power over what they create; a key aspect of "platform power" is indeed the "endogenous" nature of their capacity to engender the environment and conditions in which markets and societal sectors around them must function . But they do retain dependencies with actors who they need legitimation and governance frameworks from, particularly those that recommend which basic norms alignment ought to integrate. These may be epistemic (definitions of "misinformation" and other types of information integrity); social (representational, political or other); physical (personal or societal); and other more "essentially contested" questions that call for public deliberation to be integrated into alignment processes. Value alignment is, in this sense, a site of power struggle and deliberation.

      It is also a site that has not been studied empirically, and studying it requires novel methods. Part of this is a question of inclusive design. Who is consulted about alignment norms, what is their participation (and applicability) in alignment processes, and how does one highlight contestation where it occurs? Another is a question of negotiation — or indeed of struggle, should we acquiesce the irredeemably diverse interests of AI governance stakeholders. How are different actors invited to deliberate and collaborate in alignment ? Much has been said of the struggle to open algorithmic systems to a wider market or to regulatory frameworks , with middleware and algorithmic pluralism being a few of the latest conceptual propositions brought to the table of policy makers seeking some form of "collaborative responsibility" . Here, AI companies speak of select partnerships with academic, governmental and other experts in vulnerable fields; of red-teaming networks and communities ; and at times attempts to consult mini-publics about which risks and values to steer alignment with, as has been the case with OpenAI's collective alignment initiative , Anthropic's public constitutional AI and Google's STELA programme .

      Still, it is difficult to tell outside of AI companies what participation or deliberation means beyond occasional consultation processes and specific partnerships, let alone whether the term speaks to how scholars have used to imply a distribution of agency over the purpose, design or consequences of a technical system . We cannot assume that a survey, citation or invitation to red team a model constitutes participation in this latter sense, nor that deliberative processes are structured in the way intended by interventions in deliberative theory since Habermas . What we can infer from trails of alignment documentation is, beyond the kinds of values and risks companies pick and define, what kind of actor is cited, acknowledged or included in their definition, in data curation, training and benchmarking. Through citation patterns, we try to deduce not just which actors do what, but how integrated or "enrolled" each of them is throughout alignment processes .

      Here lies the methodological novelty of this article: we look beyond just the realm of policy writing and towards all the components that make the stack of alignment. Studies of platform governance, and much of what has been written on AI governance so far, tend to depart from policies and interviews. Alignment, however, is assembled across documents of many kinds — frontier governance frameworks, model and system cards, constitutions and model specifications, framework implementation reports, public policy positions, partnerships, programmes, evaluations and essays — each of which does a different kind of normative work and enrols a different set of actors. We look into actors that are part of AI governance, notably of value alignment, and we ask which actors partake in alignment, and what the nature and impact of their decision-making is.

      Concretely, we perform a distant reading of alignment-related documents associated with AI companies across the United States, Europe and China. We speak of a distant reading because we look for specific datapoints in these documents: the names and types of actors (nonprofits, governmental, internal, private, academic); how they are cited; and what process they are associated to, from the definition of alignment values, to data curation, trainings and benchmarking. This is, to our knowledge, a novel analysis of alignment documents: not an audit of their completeness, but a reading of the relations they record.

      Though these documents are of course not representative of everything that occurs in alignment processes (and everyone involved), they do in fact participate in the governance process of AI companies by performing public duties towards "responsible democratization of machine learning" , responding to the sensitivities of regulators and public debates about the prospective use of their models, and position themselves in a wider market of other alignment norms and technical performance benchmarks. No matter how technical, these documents (and those they cite) are effectively sites of deliberation in their own right. They cite conceptual influences from broader public debate, nonprofits with whom they have worked to benchmark or red team their models, public consultation processes, or AI safety think tanks with whom (or in response to whom) they have determined a particular alignment norm. Of course, the corpus records what companies and affiliated authors choose to disclose, in genres with different audiences and incentives; it cannot make private meetings, rejected recommendations nor uncredited labour visible. We are also cautious about deducing the motivation of an AI company to involve a given actor, even more so about estimating the impact they may have. We restrict ourselves to analysing the ways in which each actor is cited, and thus indirectly "enrolled" in a company's alignment process.

      We find that, while multiple actors are involved, each is allocated to specific and selective roles: governmental actors as initiators of "alignment standards" and producers of AI security norms and benchmarks; academics as benchmark producers and legitimisers of performance metrics; nonprofits as a hybrid category of value formulators and intermediaries with public consultation processes; and private firms concentrated in cybersecurity and red-teaming. We find, more precisely, that actors are allocated into roles depending on their ideation power: the capacity they have to define and foresight AI values and risks, given the anticipatory nature of AI governance. While some contents of current alignment methods have been subject to more collaborative endeavours (e.g., collective constitutional AI), the process itself (and its initiative) continues to be at the discretion of AI companies. We also find that safety criteria, red teaming and benchmarking stand out as frequent entry points for nonprofits, think tanks, academics and regulators to propose alignment criteria within a nascent market of "independent verification organisations" . These organisations work and compete to offer companies ideation services: the joint capacity to formulate new values, norms and risks peculiar to AI use and development, relative to modelled scenarios in which current AIs may affect key societal sectors. Conversely, actors that are typically more influential in platform governance do not reappear here, perhaps because they do not exert this kind of ideation power. We close by reflecting on what this distribution of roles implies for the political theory of AI governance, and what alternative models of legitimacy a more pluralistic alignment infrastructure might require.

      The actors of alignment governance

      Before we explore where and which actors intervene in alignment, it is worth locating the notion of alignment itself. Though its use may not always enjoy universal consensus , alignment describes the effort by which AI models are steered into generating information in one or another way based on a number of trained norms. There are prompts that cause models not to generate any information at all — refusals — and others that lead to them using more or less predictable discursive techniques in support or opposition to one or another norm. This applies not just in chatbots, but in the integration of models in a variety of information sorting techniques across the Web, to think of search and recommendations.

      With alignment comes the art of translating "human intentions and values" into machine behaviour , relative to how humans themselves specify those items in time, space and dialogue . In this regard, alignment does not differ much from decades of work in values in design. The practical difference, here, may lie in the specific affordances left for external actors to deliberate and input their own values into AI models — and not just at the stage of a policy design . We speak indeed of interventions across the AI "lifecycle" , from the specification of risks and values, data brokerage, access and curation, training methods, benchmarking and other forms of evaluation.

      Alignment as a stack

      Thus far, alignment tends to be located as a distinct "modality" of governance among others. Veale, Matus and Gorwa situate ethics councils, industry governance, contracts and licensing, standards, and international agreements and extraterritorial law as parallel modalities through which AI is governed . These can likewise be mapped as different normative arenas of "AI control" : transhumanist superintelligence and alignment speculation; researcher self-certification science; fundamental-rights critiques; and product-safety and risk regulation — an argument which holds that the EU AI Act covers only a narrow slice of them.

      Within companies, alignment is best contextualised — as in — within a greater "alignment stack": an interdependent set of norms from legislation, conventions or standards, which become alignment frameworks (acceptable use policies, frontier model frameworks, constitutions, model cards, partnerships, initiatives), which may be enforced via LLM behaviour (discursive techniques and others), which meet user circumventions, infrastructures, and so on.

      Different actors practice different forms of decision-making with different powers along it. One can also see the normative side of this problem as "six alignment targets" — instructions, expressed intentions, revealed preferences, informed preferences, interest or well-being, and values . In practice, we see the following.

      Specification: the formalisation of values within specific boundaries , with the understanding that AI behaviours can be exponentially unpredictable in exponentially complex settings . Specification sets definitions and criteria for success in different scenarios, also based on training data. This also means picking and determining values based on their estimated or potential impact — which is in itself an estimation, a guess, more than a reported harm. It involves deciding what the model is aligned to, where the options include instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests, or values ; whose values count, and how conflicts among them are resolved, with choices ranging from a single global standard, to personalization within bounds, to pluralistic or context-specific norms ; and how principles are written and at what level of generality, which means choosing between broad values and operational rules, and deciding how to rank helpfulness, harmlessness and honesty when they conflict .

      In computer science literature, the definition of ideal conducts and risks in AIs is sometimes known as specification . It means specifying the intents, values and risks by which an AI ought to speak and behave presently and prospectively, particularly in response to concerns it may eventually misalign with initial precepts. Goethe's tale of the sorcerer's apprentice comes to mind : less than a rule-based computational or other mechanism, an AI detains the learning and (thus) creative capacities to extrapolate what it was originally intended to do. The sorcerer's apprentice fails to specify the exact conditions in which brooms ought to fetch water, and by the time hundreds of brooms inundate the room, they can no longer tell how and under what conditions to stop executing their initial command. In these terms, alignment ought to act as an anchor that pre-empts loss of control and other transgressions, be these "formal oracles" that restrict AIs to "scoped roles" (for example, limiting LLMs to being chatbots) , baking restrictions into their hardware, or determining minimum threshold "red lines" enforced by legislation instead of non-binding principles, policies, or institutional conventions .

      Education and optimisation: writing examples of good responses with which a model is fine-tuned (SFT, supervised fine-tuning); choosing, ranking or contrasting preferences in answers (RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or DPO, direct preference optimisation); principle-based training, where a written set of principles guides a model to pick normative ideals (constitutional AI, RLAIF); and collective and pluralist methods, where principles and preferences come from deliberation between mini publics.

      Anticipation: testing how well an AI performs in different, unpredictable scenarios, as a form of "anticipatory governance" , which also needs to be taken into account across different settings and sectors. Anticipation is the component that most distinguishes alignment from content moderation. A moderation regime classifies material that already exists; alignment must decide what a model may do in situations that have not yet arisen, and must do so before deployment, when the evidence for a harm is a projection rather than a record. This is why the frontier governance frameworks in our corpus are written as conditionals: they name capability or risk thresholds, the evaluations by which a threshold is detected, and the safeguards that follow if it is reached. Google DeepMind sets "critical capability levels", OpenAI distinguishes "high" from "critical" capability, Anthropic assigns AI Safety Levels, Microsoft combines a compute threshold with qualitative capability thresholds, and xAI reserves a judgement of "significance and severity". Each is an attempt to make a future state actionable in the present, and each requires someone to model that future state convincingly. That requirement is what gives alignment research organisations their position: an organisation that can turn "scheming", "sandbagging" or an autonomy "time horizon" into a demonstrable scenario supplies the object a threshold can be written about. Anticipation is therefore where ideation power is exercised most directly, and where our corpus shows external actors closest to the definition of what counts as a risk at all.

      Evaluation: validating LLM behaviour under various criteria via benchmarks or other evaluation mechanisms — a source of legitimacy. Evaluation converts a norm into a number, and in doing so decides what will count as evidence that a norm has been honoured. It is the most open component of the stack, because a test can travel: an external party can write a benchmark, or run one against a model, without being given any authority over training. This modularity explains both the size of the evaluation market and its limits. Companies retain the choice of which benchmark to adopt, which threshold counts as passing, and what follows from a failure; external actors supply the instrument and, increasingly, the verification. The corpus separates two capacities that are easily conflated: authoring a benchmark, which is spread across academics, alignment research organisations and individuals, and evaluating a company's model, which is concentrated in a small set of organisations given pre-release access — METR, Apollo Research, Gray Swan, SecureBio, the UK AI Security Institute. Evaluation also carries a legitimising function that exceeds its technical one: a published score demonstrates that a value has been rendered verifiable, whether or not the value was contested in the first place.

      Applications, called "beneficial deployments" by companies: where and how to evaluate the use, application and impact of AIs in a given setting, such as education or politics. This component treats alignment as something to be judged in situ rather than in the abstract: a model is aligned to the extent that its use in a hospital, a classroom or a public administration produces the intended effects. It brings a different cast of actors into the documents — health institutes, education ministries, national laboratories, domain foundations — and a different register of evidence, since the claims concern outcomes in a sector rather than behaviour in a test. It is also the component where the boundary between alignment and market expansion is thinnest: the same partnership that supplies domain expertise about what a model should refuse also opens a sector to the product. We keep the distinction in the taxonomy, coding sector deployments separately from partnerships whose stated object is alignment, precisely because the documents themselves often blur it.

      Documentation: what is released for evaluation, including transparency reports. Documentation is not a neutral record of the other components but a governance mechanism of its own . It determines what is available to be contested: a value that is never written down cannot be argued with, and an evaluation that is never reported cannot be replicated. Documentation also does the work of standardisation, since a genre that is repeated — the model card, the system card, the framework report — turns otherwise arbitrary internal decisions into a convention against which the next document is read. This is why the documentary taxonomy set out below is more than an organising device for our corpus: the genres differ in what they bind, in whom they address and in which actors they name, and the movement of a norm between genres — from a constitution into a system card, from a framework into an implementation report — is itself one of the relations we can observe.

      The allocation of decision-making

      Thus far, the question of actor participation has been primarily examined as one of optional consultation. Delgado et al., for example, assemble a corpus of 80 articles and 12 practitioner interviews and find that participation is "largely consultative" and rarely grants design-decision agency . The same holds in Rakova et al., Madaio et al. and Widder et al. Huang et al. sourced principles from 1,002 representative US adults who contributed 1,127 statements and cast 38,252 votes via Polis, and trained a "Public" constitution model . Companion programmes include OpenAI's Democratic Inputs to AI and Collective Alignment, Meta's Community Forums with the Stanford Deliberative Democracy Lab, and Google's STELA , with PRISM as the empirical-participation counterpart . In all cases, developers tend to retain control of transforming raw input into principles .

      Google's STELA framework makes the division of labour particularly visible. Forty-four participants from four communities discussed pre-selected chatbot interactions, before researchers coded their statements, formulated provisional rules, harmonised overlapping items and submitted the ruleset to expert review . The process therefore elicits situated knowledge that developer rulesets tend to miss, including context around impartiality, factuality and culturally specific harms. But the authors themselves stress that participants neither selected the dialogue samples nor directly proposed the final rules, and did not control the study's artefacts. Participation consequently affects the material available for curation while the passage from discussion to a training-ready ruleset remains organised by researchers.

      Another popular lens through which the question of participation has been examined is crowdwork, particularly in annotation and red teaming. Here, the judgment of the participant becomes alignment data capable of affecting the model. They do not, however, determine the composition of the worker pool, the interface and instructions, the models to which they are assigned, or how helpfulness and harmlessness are weighted against one another . On external red teaming and flaw disclosure, for example, Ahmad et al. describe campaigns in which a company selects external experts, determines their level of access, provides interfaces and documentation requirements, and then synthesises the resulting observations . Correspondingly, Cattell et al. push for coordinated flaw disclosure for AI beyond security vulnerabilities , while others maintain that in-house evaluation is insufficient relative to third-party evaluation, standardised flaw reports and legal safe harbours — something AI companies have been tentatively addressing .

      Alignment research organisations

      A particular class of organisation that tend to bypass exclusion — due in part to their decision-making capacities — is alignment research organisations. A scholarly anchor for a definition may be found in Raji et al.'s Outsider Oversight . In it, we recognise organisations like METR, who conducts autonomous-capability and "time-horizon" evaluations and spun off from ARC Evals in December 2023; Redwood Research, which focuses on AI "control" protocols robust to deceptive models; and Apollo Research, which focuses on evaluating "scheming" and strategic deception.

      Still, the literature shows that external actors are mostly invited to consult on alignment values and AI risks and do not hold decision-making power across the stack . Kallina et al. identify a disconnect between responsible-AI guidance, where participation is often justified as a redistribution of agency, and industry practice, where developers, executives and legal teams retain control over the scope of stakeholder involvement . Third-party actors appear primarily as "expert red teamers", people invited for their authoritative right to identify a key value or risk, or as alignment research organisations specialising in specification and ideation: defining AI norms, modelling their risks and turning them into evaluations.

      This raises what becomes the organising question of the article: has the participatory turn called for in AI design reached alignment as a whole? Our addition to Kallina et al. is to examine this question across alignment processes, rather than through the guidance of documents and practitioner accounts alone. This, in turn, allows us to examine another participation in the process of operationalisation: that is, from the point of defining alignment values to that of doing data curation, training and benchmarking.

      Method

      There are points from which we can begin an empirical research project: platform governance and what has been written on AI governance so far, both of which tend to depart from policies and interviews. What kind of data do we have here? Lots, with lots of possibilities depending on what part of alignment we are looking at — which is why we begin from the alignment stack rather than from a single genre of text.

      Thus far, studies have looked at alignment documents — especially model cards — primarily through the lens of rigour and partisanship. Liang et al. survey model card diligence across AI companies and find that only 44.2% of models had a model card (though they accounted for 90.5% of total download traffic), and that informativeness was uneven: environmental-impact, limitations and evaluation sections were least completed, and later work notes fewer than 17% of cards carried substantive risk content . Others, like Bommasani et al., look at the quality of model card writing: the Foundation Model Transparency Index scores developers on 100 indicators across upstream (data, labour, compute), model, and downstream domains, measured over time across companies .

      On closer look, we may find that alignment documents actively participate in alignment themselves. What can be different in kind from social media and other platforms is that — as a platform artefact of sorts — AI systems like these aggregate data to an exponentially unpredictable effect, of both substance (the nature of the effect) and dimension (the gravity, severity). This requires a different mode of governance, set and configuration of actors and governance techniques, which alignment documents formalise in different steps and with different discursive techniques.

      Table 1. The alignment document taxonomy.

      Principles

      "Governing documents" over the training of a company's models, its "controls", "monitoring systems" and the "organisational culture" that underpins it: the document that takes precedence over any other. Principles are deontological, setting grounds for how AI's role in society should be (for example, "AI should serve humanity"), and setting the absolute behavioural boundaries of AI and its conditions for action. They state values and risks, and "fundamental root rules", "red lines" or "constraints". Their practical effect is the field of action of a company and the values that flow downstream from it as "chains of command". It is intended to speak to broader audiences in academia, governance and legal sector.

      Frontier governance frameworks

      A "preparedness framework" intended to clarify technical and conceptual mitigation strategies to severe risks. It contains a list of core risks and their definitions; mitigation strategies for each severe risk; and frontier developer obligations — the legal obligations a company is responding to, the strategy with which a company responds to these obligations (for example transparency reports), as well as how they open up the company to independent evaluation. It also contains risk hierarchies and probabilities.

      Examples include: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (2023, ASL levels); OpenAI's Preparedness Framework (2023 beta; v2 April 2025); Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (2024, "critical capability levels"); Meta's Frontier AI Framework — synthesised by METR's Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies (2024).

      Model behaviour specifications

      Model cards perform a standardization of reporting otherwise arbitrary decisions in training, which then contributes to a convention, norm and standardization. As part of a normative infrastructure, they are the artefacts the corpus is built on, and they frame documentation as an accountability device . Model specs and constitutions, in turn, work as public-facing governance: OpenAI's Model Spec, Anthropic's Claude's Constitution. Together, they are documents that specify the larger boundaries of a model's behaviour (for example Claude's Constitution) that specify what the model should and should not be and do, including the tone of the agent, values, fundamental root rules that cannot be over-written, and a hierarchy of instructions.

      Public policy positions

      Taking a position with regards to contemporary AI issues; announcements and introductions of new programmes; and so on. This is the genre that most resembles the material platform-governance research usually works with, and the one in which companies speak as political actors rather than as developers. Its addressee is an institution — a legislature, an agency, an administration — and its claims are demands on others rather than commitments of the company's own, though the same document often pairs a demand with a demonstration that the company already meets it. In the corpus (20 documents) they take five recurring forms: endorsing or opposing a bill, as Anthropic does with SB 53; submitting recommendations to an administration, as in the recommendations to OSTP or the response to a governor's working group; proposing an instrument the state should adopt, as in third-party testing as a key ingredient of AI policy, a framework for AI development transparency, or the teen and child safety blueprints; taking a position on a contested technical-political question, such as open-weights models or export controls; and setting out a company's reading of the economic and security stakes, as in the Economic Policy Framework or the Policy on the AI Exponential. Data points: addressee (institution, jurisdiction); bill or docket; position; date.

      Usage policies and terms

      Rules for users and deployers rather than for the company or the model: acceptable-use policies, usage policies, consumer terms, and the guidelines issued to organisations serving particular populations (5 documents). The decision rule is that the addressee is the user or the customer. They enter this study only where they inform how a model is trained, moderated or filtered, and they are distinguished from safeguards explainers, which describe how such rules are enforced.

      Model documentation

      System cards, model cards, addenda, technical reports, training-data summaries and application cards (91 documents, the largest genre after research). Each documents one model, model family or product: its training, capabilities, evaluations and limitations. This is where external evaluators are most often named, and where the norms written elsewhere — in a constitution, a principle, a framework — appear in operational form, attached to a test and a result. It is distinguished from framework implementation reports, which assess a model against a named framework's thresholds rather than describing the model as such.

      Framework implementation reports

      Documents that apply a named frontier framework to a model or to a moment (12 documents): Frontier Safety Framework reports for a particular Gemini model, Anthropic's redacted risk reports and alignment risk updates, the activation of ASL-3 protections, OpenAI's safety overview for a new model and its individual misalignment reports. The decision rule is that the document cites a framework threshold and reports a determination against it. These are the documents in which anticipation is settled in practice, and the genre in which alignment research organisations appear most often as evaluators.

      Misuse and threat intelligence reports

      Reports of observed misuse of the company's systems by identified or unidentified actors, and of the enforcement taken (8 documents). Unlike safeguards explainers, they describe cases rather than measures: a campaign, an operator, a set of accounts, a disruption. Data points: period covered; actor types; countries; harm domains; enforcement actions.

      Transparency and compliance reports

      Periodic or standing accounts of a company's governance practice as a whole, often keyed to commitments or to regulation (11 documents): Microsoft's Responsible AI Transparency Reports, Anthropic's Transparency Hub and its voluntary-commitments tracker, statements of alignment with the EU AI Act. They differ from certifications in being self-reported, and from model documentation in covering the company rather than one model.

      Certifications and third-party assessments

      Assessments performed or certified by a party other than the company (6 documents): ISO/IEC 42001 certification, SOC 2 reports, cross-laboratory evaluation exercises such as the Anthropic–OpenAI pilot, and playbooks for third-party evaluation. The decision rule is that an external party issues or conducts the assessment. This genre is small but analytically important, since it is the only one in which the company is the object rather than the author of the reading.

      Statements and incident disclosures

      Documents that respond to a specific event — a government action, a dispute, an incident — and are dated to it (9 documents). They are distinguished from public policy positions, which state general positions on regulation rather than reacting to an occurrence.

      Commitments, unilateral and multilateral

      Documents in which a company undertakes a future obligation, alone or with others (11 documents): signing the EU Code of Practice, the White House voluntary commitments, the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, child-safety commitments with Thorn and All Tech Is Human, commitments on model deprecation and preservation. The decision rule is an explicit "we commit" or "we sign". They differ from frontier frameworks in not being threshold-based, and from partnerships in creating an obligation rather than a project.

      Partnerships and MOUs

      Bilateral or multilateral agreements with a named external party around a project or an issue (37 documents): memoranda with governments, collaborations with AI safety institutes, agreements with national laboratories, sector partnerships in health and education. Data points: counterparty type (state, NGO, firm, AI safety institute); country; sector; instrument.

      Governance bodies and structures

      Documents that create or describe a standing body which shapes company decisions or sector coordination (12 documents): the Long-Term Benefit Trust, advisory councils on national security or economics, expert councils on well-being, company institutes, and inter-firm bodies such as the Frontier Model Forum. They are distinguished from partnerships by not being bound to a project.

      Programmes and funding

      Company-run programmes that fund, invite or grant access to external actors (30 documents): grants and fellowships, bug bounties, trusted-access and cyber-defence programmes, research funds. Grant programmes carry the research-capture critiques discussed above , since they determine which external questions are askable at all.

      Safeguards and product-safety explainers

      Descriptions of a mitigation, safeguard or product-safety measure the company has built or applied (46 documents) — classifiers, refusal behaviour, age prediction, parental controls, election safeguards, watermarking. They describe the measure rather than the rule it enforces (usage policies) or the knowledge behind it (research).

      Research

      Papers, preprints and research blog posts that advance knowledge on an AI issue, typically with authors and methods (120 documents, the largest genre): alignment faking, sycophancy, sleeper agents, reward hacking, interpretability, model welfare, the consultation of publics. They are distinguished from evaluations and tools, which release a reusable instrument rather than report a finding.

      Evaluations, benchmarks and tools

      Releases of a reusable instrument for measuring or auditing models (16 documents): a benchmark, an evaluation suite, an open-source auditing tool such as Petri or Bloom, a leaderboard. Data points: what is measured; open or closed; licence; adoption by others.

      Essays and vision statements

      Documents in which leadership articulates views on AI's future, ethics or societal role without committing to specific actions (18 documents): the essays of Dario Amodei and Demis Hassabis, Microsoft's humanist superintelligence, OpenAI's planning for AGI. They are distinguished from public policy positions by making no concrete ask of government.

      Mapping actor participation

      The goal of this method is to find which actors "participate" in alignment and what this participation consists of. We distinguish five documentary relations. Actors may be cited, where they are credited with an alignment idea, value or process; invited, where a document says that they took part in a consultation, red team or other exercise; or co-authors, where they are named as contributors to the document itself. They may also be enrolled across several components of the alignment stack, rather than appearing in one isolated exercise. Finally, actors may be excluded in the narrower sense that organisations active in the wider alignment field are never mentioned in the company documents studied here. These relations do not by themselves prove causal influence but may show the capacities in which companies publicly make other actors part of alignment.

      In comparison to content moderation studies primarily focused on policy documents, tackling this empirically means charting AI alignment as an interdependent stack of technical and normative interventions. There is the question of what values to train: their definition and provenance, from legislation, research or public norms; how they trickle down into specific "ideal conducts" by which a model should behave, as well as risks to be mitigated; data cleanup and curation processes; the training of each conduct and mitigation of each risk; and their benchmarking and evaluation. Each component may assemble a variety of governance mechanisms : model cards, AI charters and principles, company updates in which norms are spelled out as part of transparency exercises ; consultations and forms of expertise, particularly in red teaming ; and benchmarks adopted from a wider research and evaluation market. These mechanisms tend to involve different actors in different capacities, including academics, alignment research organisations such as Apollo Research or METR, regulators, state-funded AI safety institutes, civil-society organisations, data workers and publics.

      We therefore treat participation as a relation that can move, or fail to move, across these alignment goals. A public may be invited to formulate a principle but disappear when data are curated. An academic laboratory may be cited as the author of a benchmark without participating in training. An external evaluator may inspect a model without deciding whether it is released. The procedures below trace these differences rather than counting every mention as an equal share of decision-making power.

      Sampling and data collection

      We sampled developers of widely used general-purpose models with publicly accessible documentation across the United States, Europe and China. The corpus centres on OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI and Microsoft in the United States; Mistral, Aleph Alpha, Almawave, Black Forest Labs and Unbabel in Europe; and Alibaba/Qwen and DeepSeek in China. The collection is consequently not a representative sample of all model developers but a comparison of companies for which enough public material exists to reconstruct at least part of an alignment process.

      We define alignment documents by what they disclose about the normative or technical ordering of model conduct. Model cards were originally proposed as a means of reporting intended uses, limitations and disaggregated evaluations . Charters, principles and constitutions officialise a normative corpus around which models ought to operate and, in some cases, be trained. Model and system cards also disclose relations with external actors whose research is cited, whose benchmarks are adopted or who are commissioned to evaluate a model. Grants, announcements and initiatives disclose programmes, collaborators and funding relationships; audits and reports document assessment; research publications describe concepts, methods or evaluations; and company policies establish public boundaries for model use and operation.

      Collection proceeded in two movements. The first was manual: documents were collected from company websites, GitHub repositories, Hugging Face attachments and locally archived PDFs or webpages between 28 January and 9 May 2026, combining 181 Zotero records with 53 company-policy snapshots from the Open Terms Archive, retaining the latest dated snapshot for each product and policy type. The second was systematic: from each company's own index pages — newsrooms, transparency hubs, model-card indexes, research listings, safety and policy sections — we crawled every linked document, recording its name, type and source section, and selected those that meet at least one of our criteria (they describe how models are trained; they articulate an alignment framework or other normative governance; they describe partnerships with external actors; or they otherwise speak to the questions above). This second movement is what allows the corpus to follow the stack rather than a genre: it captures the frontier governance frameworks, framework implementation reports, model behaviour specifications, programmes and partnership announcements that a policy-centred collection would leave out.

      Published PDFs are kept as published. Web pages are rendered to paginated PDFs, with a Markdown extraction kept alongside for reading, so that every document in the corpus is page-addressable and every finding can be cited to a page of a file that can be reopened. Documents whose file is byte-identical to another are recorded as duplicates and indexed once.

      Model cards and policies are governance artefacts, but for this same reason they record what companies choose to disclose, or are able to. Gaps may themselves be informative, though the dataset cannot always distinguish non-participation from non-disclosure. It therefore reproduces asymmetries across the industry: well-documented companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google are over-represented relative to companies that publish less.

      A taxonomy of alignment documents

      Because the corpus now spans genres, we first read around five documents per company closely and built a taxonomy of document types from that reading. Each type is defined by what binds whom: organisational principles, standards and codes bind the organisation; model behaviour specifications state a written target for model behaviour; frontier governance frameworks commit the company to if-then actions tied to capability or risk thresholds; model documentation describes one model or product; framework implementation reports apply a named framework to a model or moment; usage policies and terms address users and deployers; misuse and threat intelligence reports describe observed misuse; transparency and compliance reports account for governance practice as a whole; certifications and third-party assessments are issued or conducted by another party; statements and incident disclosures respond to a specific event; public policy positions address governments; commitments undertake future obligations; partnerships and MOUs record bilateral agreements; governance bodies and structures create standing bodies; programmes and funding fund, invite or grant access to external actors; safeguards and product-safety explainers describe a mitigation; research advances knowledge; evaluations, benchmarks and tools release reusable instruments; and essays and vision statements articulate leadership views without committing to specific actions. Landing pages that merely link to documents of several types are recorded as source sections rather than as documents.

      The taxonomy is what makes the comparison in this article possible: it lets us ask not only which actors appear, but in which kind of document they appear, and therefore in which register of governance they are being enrolled.

      Data points

      Each document yields several kinds of data points: the document itself; ideal conducts by which models ought to behave; risks they ought to mitigate; the data-curation, training or mitigation method to which each conduct or risk is connected; the benchmarks used to evaluate it; and the actors associated with each of these points. For every document, we record the authoring company and named contributors, actors explicitly mentioned in passages that define a conduct or risk, actors involved in data curation or training, benchmark authors and external evaluators.

      Not every citation is treated as an attribution of influence. A paper can be cited to recognise prior work without being credited with defining the value or risk in question. An actor is therefore coded as a specific definer only where the sentence or surrounding passage makes that relation explicit. The same rule applies elsewhere: an actor is connected to data curation or training only where the document says that it supplied data, performed red teaming, developed the technique or executed the intervention. Benchmark authors and commissioned evaluators are recorded separately because designing a test and evaluating a company's model are not the same capacity.

      Retrieval-grounded reading

      The corpus is large enough to make sequential close reading difficult, but small enough to return repeatedly to individual documents. We therefore approach it through distant reading: using computational means to process a collection in discrete but meaningful segments that hold its recurring relations in view .

      Where the earlier version of this study asked a language model to read each document end to end and compared two such readings against one another, we now ground the reading in retrieval. Every document is segmented into passages that never cross a page boundary, and indexed twice: lexically, for exact names such as benchmark titles and organisations, and semantically, for the descriptive questions that name no term in particular. A question about a data point is put to the corpus, the passages that answer it are retrieved with their page numbers, and the extraction is made from those passages alone. Each data point is therefore tied to a passage and a page in a file that can be reopened, rather than to a model's recollection of a long document.

      Retrieval is complemented by an exhaustive pass, because ranking cannot guarantee completeness: every page of every document is scanned for every actor, benchmark and training-method name in the project's vocabulary, so that a name occurring once in an appendix is not lost to a ranked list. The two passes answer different questions — retrieval finds the relation, the exhaustive scan bounds what could have been missed.

      LLMs invite a particular reflection on how much reading is delegated, as in what portion of the reading task is given to the model, what portion is retained by the analyst, and how both form a co-reading. The model selects candidate passages, extracts named actors and their relations, and maps these to a fixed schema. For every substantive finding, it is required to return a verbatim passage and page reference. The analyst determines the schema, writes the category distinctions, returns to those passages during validation and harmonises the final vocabulary. The combination of the two is what constitutes a "co-reading", in the sense that they each read documents in different but complementary distances.

      The extraction of data points proceeded in two steps. First, the model collected the document's own expressions of ideal conducts, risks, training or mitigation methods, benchmarks and participating actors. Second, these expressions were categorised manually into controlled vocabularies. Conduct categories include Honesty, Helpfulness, Harmlessness, Anti-discrimination, Political neutrality, Factuality, Reasoning, Safety and Knowledge, while risk categories include CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear risks), Cybersecurity, Bias, Sycophancy, Reward hacking, Persuasion, Misalignment, Hallucinations, Child Safety and Jailbreaking, among others. Where no category fits, the item remains under Other.

      Training and mitigation methods are mapped to eight categories: preference learning (RLHF, RLAIF, DPO and reward modelling); supervised training (SFT, instruction tuning, demonstration fine-tuning and deliberative alignment); reward shaping (adversarial reward functions, reward capping and rule-based rewards); data curation (filtering, deduplication, synthetic data and PII redaction); adversarial training (red-teaming data and adversarial examples); inference-time filtering (content classifiers, safety filters and moderation APIs); hard constraints (system-prompt rules, refusal policies and hardcoded refusals); and runtime safeguards (trip wires, model lookahead, runtime monitoring, scratchpad oversight and sandboxing).

      Actors were first assigned one of nine broad types: internal, private, academic, research institute, governmental, nonprofit, public, public consultation or other. In a second instance, we applied finer categories used in the analysis and cited literature, namely: academic research centres, alignment research organisations, government agencies, other AI companies, red-teaming or cybersecurity companies, think tanks, advocacy groups, foundations, publics, standards bodies, industry associations, coalitions and company initiatives. Citation relations were likewise normalised to distinguish document authorship, normative influence, benchmark contribution, external evaluation, red teaming, formal partnership, regulatory frameworks, coalitions, company initiatives and grant relationships. This matters because the same organisation can be an author in one document, a benchmark provider in another and an evaluator in a third.

      Validation

      Validation occurs at three levels. At the source level, verbatim passages and page references permit us to check what the model extracted and to correct mistaken relations manually; because every extraction is now made from retrieved passages, the check is against the same passages the reading used. At the category level, extracted labels and actor names are harmonised against controlled vocabularies; values outside them are reviewed rather than accepted as new categories. At a comparative level, we retain the earlier readings as baselines and report agreement between them, so that the change of instrument is itself visible in the results.

      Retrieval quality is measured separately, since a reading grounded in retrieval can only be as complete as what retrieval returns. Against page references drawn from the corrected baseline, the retrieval used here places the correct page among the first five results for roughly five out of six queries, and among the first ten for close to nine out of ten. Exact names are found more reliably than free-form descriptions of conduct and risk, which is the expected failure mode and the reason for the exhaustive scan.

      Enrolment and exclusion

      Actor-network theory (ANT) supplies the vocabulary through which we interpret "enrolment". ANT begins by treating the social not as a fixed domain populated by already stable actors, but as an effect of relations between heterogeneous entities . Companies, researchers and publics can act in a network, but so can policies, datasets, benchmarks and models insofar as other actors make them consequential. An actor's capacity is therefore relational: it depends on whether its claims can be translated into a principle, dataset, training method, score or decision that another actor can carry forward. "Enrolment", here, is our attempt to measure the extent to which each actor is involved throughout each of these steps. For every normalised actor, we record whether it appears in the definition of an ideal conduct or risk, in data curation, in another training or mitigation method, or in benchmarking and external evaluation. We also record how it appears: as a passing citation, a normative influence, a document author, a benchmark contributor, a red teamer, an evaluator or a formal partner.

      Enrolment becomes more meaningful when compared with some account of which actors exist outside the corpus. To measure this as a form of exclusion, we constructed a directory of value-alignment organisations of all types, including academic centres, civil-society organisations and government agencies. Validated Google Search results were gathered for queries on alignment, AI safety, red teaming, adversarial machine learning, algorithmic fairness, explainability, AI ethics, humane AI and responsible AI, combined with organisational terms such as organisation, NGO and nonprofit. Searches used English-, German- and Danish-language formulations and were run for four locations: the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany and Denmark. Up to 50 results were collected for each search-and-location combination.

      Of 7,377 results, 512 organisations were retained as in scope. We manually retained alignment research organisations, public-consultation organisations, advocacy groups, coalitions, industry associations, academic centres, think tanks, foundations, legal-advocacy organisations, red-teaming or cybersecurity companies and government agencies. AI developers, individuals, publishers, news outlets, generic platforms and one-off events were excluded. Their names and acronyms were compared with actor names in the alignment-document corpus using conservative exact and long-string matches. Here, exclusion means absence from the public documentary network, not proof that an organisation has never interacted privately with a company. Google rankings, query selection, languages and countries also delimit who becomes discoverable. The directory supplies a wider search horizon against which to ask which kinds of actors cross into company-facing documents and which remain largely outside them.

      Visualisations as analysis

      Three further procedures treat the corpus itself as the object of analysis rather than as a container of extracted rows.

      The first asks what discourse each document type hones. For every type in the taxonomy, we score the words and phrases that occur in a larger share of its documents than in the rest of the corpus, using a log-odds ratio with an informative prior, so that the result is not simply the vocabulary common to all AI documents. A phrase counts only if more than one company uses it, which keeps a single company's house style from standing in for a genre.

      The second asks how documents hold each other up. Documents cite one another: a model card names the constitution whose norms it operationalises, a framework implementation report names the framework whose thresholds it applies. We record an edge wherever one document links to another or names it by title, discount the links that a company's own navigation places on every page, and read the resulting network for which documents are most central, most cited, or most resourceful to others.

      The third asks who appears in each type of document and in what capacity. For every actor named on a page, we read the sentence in which the name occurs and label the relation it states — red teaming, external evaluation, benchmark authorship, standard setting, regulation, funding, partnership, data and annotation work, advice, the consultation of publics, or research — and aggregate these to document type. The authoring company and its own products are not counted as actors in their own documents.

      Limitations

      A few limitations are worth mentioning. First, it is known that model card adoption remains uneven. A study of more than 32,000 model cards finds substantial variation in what developers document, with limitations and evaluation details often left incomplete . Only 15.4% include an evaluation section and 17.4% a limitations section, while results tend to be reported in aggregate rather than for specific subgroups . Second, one must accept that any form of natural language processing trades exhaustive human reading for coverage. Grounding the reading in retrieval changes the shape of this trade rather than removing it: a relation stated in a passage that retrieval does not return is not read, which is why the exhaustive name scan is run alongside it, and why retrieval quality is reported as part of the results. Third, controlled vocabularies are necessarily incomplete, though they have grown iteratively as the documents are read manually. The benchmark taxonomy in particular collapses substantively different evaluations into a single category where their published descriptions are too brief to disambiguate (for example, internal company suites that share a name but differ in content from one model version to the next). Fourth, rendering a web page to a paginated file fixes it at the date of capture; live pages change, and page numbers in rendered documents depend on that layout. Finally, the exclusion directory is geographically and linguistically bounded and dependent on Google Search. Its 512 actors do not constitute the entire alignment field, particularly outside Europe and the US.

      Actors and values

      The actors assembled in alignment documents are more heterogeneous than a division between companies and their stakeholders would suggest. Internal actors include the companies themselves, their model teams, safety and preparedness teams, policy staff, researchers and named employees. Governmental actors include general regulatory bodies, executive offices and declarations, as well as the more specialised AI Safety Institutes that evaluate frontier models. Academic actors range from individual scholars to universities and research centres cited for a concept, method or benchmark. Private actors include other AI companies, data and infrastructure providers, cybersecurity firms and red-teaming companies. Publics appear as users, crowdworkers, annotators, survey respondents and participants in consultation processes. Civil society is more variegated still: it includes advocacy organisations, foundations, think tanks, public-consultation organisations and a particular class of alignment research organisations, such as Apollo Research and METR, whose main trade is the formulation and evaluation of prospective AI risks.

      One way of reading this distribution is as a distinction between "hard" and more contingent values. Cybersecurity, CBRN safety and compliance are generally rendered as threat models, legal thresholds, dangerous capabilities or operational red lines. Political neutrality, personality, diversity and even factuality are more visibly contingent on a situation, audience or account of legitimate disagreement. These are values for which the acceptable conduct is less likely to be settled by a single capability threshold and more likely to remain essentially contested, and thus needing external or simulated deliberation inputs.

      This distinction between "hard" and more contingent values may correspond to different kinds of specification power. Technological definitions frame AI in terms of computational architectures and threats that can be modelled by technical actors, while governance-oriented definitions frame it through legal applicability, compliance and risk management . As such, a "hard" legally binding definition may oblige a company to act, yet still leave open how that obligation becomes a training example, classifier or evaluation threshold. Conversely, "softer" non-binding principles developed by academic, private or civil society organisations have no equivalent legal force, but can and do circulate more easily as taxonomies, benchmarks and technical frameworks. Standards occupy another position: they establish shared vocabularies and lifecycle-oriented risk-management procedures without necessarily deciding the value itself. Credited actors reflect this division of labour. While most values remain defined in the voice of the company document, risks are more likely to receive an attribution by, other than academic researchers, governmental agencies and cybersecurity companies and red-teaming specialists. White House commitments, the US Executive Order on safe, secure and trustworthy AI and the Bletchley Declaration recur as sources of frontier-risk and security categories.

      Alignment research organisations like Apollo Research illustrates another kind of definitional work: it is credited in ten distinct risk definitions and appears repeatedly as a benchmark author and evaluator. In OpenAI's o1 documentation, Apollo defines scheming as covertly pursuing goals misaligned with those of developers or users, and then supplies an evaluation suite through which that prospective behaviour can be tested . In the GPT-4o system card, it evaluates instrumental self-modification, alignment faking and theory-of-mind tasks under the same larger category . METR performs a related operation around autonomy. It is introduced as an organisation assessing whether frontier systems could pose catastrophic societal risks and evaluates models through time horizons for completing end-to-end tasks.

      We may call this line of service a form of ideation, meaning the capacity to project, relative to a calculated and demonstrated risk, the values and frameworks within which AIs ought to be trained. Ideation tends to translate models into categories on which alignment can be produced. Alignment research organisations specialise in making such futures technically portable: "scheming", "sandbagging", "evaluation awareness" or an autonomy "time horizon" can travel from a speculative scenario into a model card, benchmark and preparedness decision. They consequently hold more impact over the specification of alignment than their lack of formal authority would suggest — that is, their norms are non-binding, but particularly translatable.

      Other organisations are building a more specific trade around the infrastructure of deliberation. OpenAI's Democratic Inputs programme solicited methods for deciding model rules and assembled public-consultation organisations, political theorists and alignment researchers around questions including personalisation, professional advice, emotional support, output diversity and the treatment of cultural and human-rights conflicts . Ovadya describes this organisational function as "democracy-as-a-service": third parties develop the expertise to convene representative deliberations and translate their outcomes for governments or companies . These processes are consequential, but their terms remain curated. In Collective Constitutional AI, Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project selected the public, set the task and removed contributions considered hateful, nonsensical, duplicative, irrelevant, poorly formatted or technically infeasible. In the Democratic Fine-Tuning trial, a designed chatbot guided participants away from rules and political slogans towards what the researchers considered underlying values, while actual fine-tuning was deferred to later work.

      Training and evaluating

      Once a value has been specified, the network contracts. Of 787 distinct training and mitigation interventions, 746, or 94.8%, name an internal company actor. External actors do appear as annotators, domain experts, publics, academic collaborators, advocacy organisations and red teamers, but generally alongside the company that selects and incorporates their contribution. There are practical reasons for this closure, as training requires access to data mixtures, model weights, reward functions, classifiers, system prompts and production infrastructure that companies do not generally expose. It is also where several objectives need reconciling, as when a public principle favouring openness may conflict with a cybersecurity refusal, or a harmlessness intervention may produce over-refusal. Deciding how examples are sampled, how losses are weighted and when a model is ready to ship consequently tends to join normative authority to infrastructural control. Security, intellectual-property and product-release considerations intensify this concentration.

      How to read this visualisation

      What is a stack?

      One stack = one group of actors (one actor type, or one company when you switch the Group dropdown). The four parallelograms inside it are the four alignment stages, top to bottom:

      1. Conducts — desired behaviours a model is trained for.
      2. Risks — harms the company names and tries to mitigate.
      3. Training — the techniques used (RLHF, SFT, filters, …).
      4. Benchmarks — the evaluations used to grade the model.

      What is a node?

      Each circle is one actor (a person, an organisation, a consortium) that appears in at least one document touching that stage.

      • Filled circles are actors who are explicitly credited at that stage (the named training actor, benchmark author, or the actor a conduct / risk is attributed to) or who authored the source document.
      • Hollow circles are actors merely cited in a document on that stage — referenced without doing the work themselves. Toggle them off with Include cited actors.

      Click any node to isolate the network it belongs to: the actor lights up wherever it appears across the four stages and the tan edges linking it between components are highlighted, while everything else dims. Click the background or press Esc to clear.

      How big is a node?

      A node's size is the relative frequency with which that actor is mentioned within that component (Conducts, Risks, Training or Benchmarks). Radius is sqrt-scaled against the most-mentioned actor on the plane, so a government agency named in many risk rows shows a bigger circle on the Risks plane than one named once.

      The same actor can therefore be a large node on one plane and a small one on another, depending on where it is named most. Position within the plane is a force-directed layout (repulsion + a weak central pull) and carries no meaning on its own — only size and the cross-stage edges do.

      Labels. By default no circles carry a permanent label. Hover any circle to reveal its actor name transiently; tick Show labels to label every node.

      When do two nodes connect?

      An edge appears when the same actor is mentioned in more than one component. For example, if a government agency is named under both Conducts and Training, it appears as a node on each plane and the two are joined by an edge. The denser an actor's edges, the more stages of the alignment stack it is enrolled in.

      There are no within-plane edges: lines only ever run between components, tracking one actor's reach up and down the stack.

      What about the tan vertical edges?

      A tan line connects the same actor appearing on two stages — i.e. it tracks an actor's enrolment through the alignment stack. Each one is split at its midpoint:

      • The half nearest the source slab is drawn behind the slab → the line disappears into it on the way out.
      • The half nearest the target slab is painted over the slab → the line emerges from it on arrival.

      Read denser tan threads as: this actor type is enrolled in more than one stage of alignment.

      Caveats

      • Each plane is capped at the top-N most-mentioned actors (configurable). Capped actors prefer explicitly-credited / author roles over cited-only.
      • Force layouts are non-deterministic across reloads; cluster membership is stable, cluster position is not.
      • An actor's type is taken from the row where they appear; the same name with different types (e.g. an academic who also founds a nonprofit) can land in different stacks. This is a feature, not a bug, but worth knowing.

Twenty of those matched entries are based in the United States, against three in the United Kingdom and one in Switzerland. The US actors are not evenly distributed across the country. Many belong to an institutional ecology in or near the Bay Area, including Stanford and UC Berkeley centres, METR, FAR.AI, Redwood Research, the Center for AI Safety and the Alignment Research Center, together with coalitions connected to the same frontier-AI industry. None of the 117 German or 54 Danish directory entries is matched, nor is any actor from the Netherlands, Belgium or France. Only three of 57 UK entries reach the corpus. In this sense, remains outside represents another potential field of ideation. The lack of participation of non-US and elite organisations tends to narrow the imaginative field of AI policy-making altogether.

      Conclusion

      This article asked what actors are involved in value alignment and how much power they exert on AI companies to deliberate, influence and decide about values across definition, data curation, training and benchmarking. Our main finding is not that external participation is absent, but that it is allocated. Governmental actors supply legal obligations, standards and security priorities. Academics supply concepts, methods and benchmarks. Private firms supply infrastructure and adversarial testing. Civil-society actors articulate rights claims and facilitate occasional public processes. Publics contribute preferences, principles, examples and attacks. Alignment research organisations supply prospective risk models, taxonomies and evaluations.

      This allocation follows the capacities and discursive affordances of each actor. Legal discourse can oblige or prohibit, but does not by itself specify a classifier or evaluation threshold. Academic and civil-society norms lack that binding force, but can travel as concepts, principles and benchmarks. Security specialists enter where a danger can be expressed as a threat model. This, as highlighted by , qualifies the participatory turn described in AI design research as largely consultative, as is consistent with findings that external actors enter after system objectives are fixed and that organisational hierarchies constrain the effect of red-teaming findings .

      Still, alignment research organisations form a partial exception because they reunite capacities otherwise distributed between several actors. They offer services of ideation: that is, to define a value, norm or risk and model a situation in which it may become desirable, consequential or any other normative projection. Their influence comes not only from technical expertise, but from assembling specification, foresight and verification into one service. These are capacities traditionally associated, in different forms, with various government agencies, to think of agenda-setting, risk assessment, standardisation and oversight. Here, alignment research organisations perform them through non-binding and translatable instruments, often with closer access to company models than public agencies possess.

      Some alignment research and civil-society organisations also act as platforms for deliberation. This raises a further institutional question: why should deliberation about generally deployed AI values be convened principally through company grants and private partnerships rather than at a governmental level? Public facilitation could make the remit, participant selection, evidence and company response answerable to institutions with a broader constituency, and connect deliberation to binding consequences. Governments could commission their forecasting, facilitation and evaluation capacities while retaining public control over the agenda, access conditions and consequences of failure. The UK AI Security Institute provides a partial model for this arrangement, though its remit remains concentrated on the "hard" security risks most readily translated into evaluations. A more complete public alignment infrastructure would extend the same institutional capacity to disputed values, affected communities and forms of harm that cannot be reduced to a benchmark.
