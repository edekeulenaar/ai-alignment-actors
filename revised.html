The actors in AI alignment

One of the major controversies AI has brought to societal debate concerns its renewed role in public access to information (Branford, Soulier and Fichtner, 2025). One once spoke of "algorithms" as a catch-all denominator for the underlying infrastructures and politics behind information management (Bowker and Star, 2000), be that as ranking systems (Rieder, Matamoros-Fernández and Coromina, 2018), newsfeeds (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2019), recommenders (Möller et al., 2018) or a variety of content moderation systems (Gillespie, 2022; Gorwa, Binns and Katzenbach, 2020). There is now an understanding that AI systems — LLMs, in particular — come to replace these as ever more intuitive and precise personalization systems. Seamless as this shift may feel, the political implications remain profound: both algorithmic and AI-based information systems condition the levers through which access to and the circulation of information are aligned with societal norms.

A central political consequence of this shift is the question of who gets to decide and operationalise societal norms into technical systems. Perhaps accidentally, AI companies have again ended up detaining most decision-making power over what they create; a key aspect of "platform power" is indeed the "endogenous" nature of their capacity to engender the environment and conditions in which markets and societal sectors around them must function (Nieborg et al., 2024). But they do retain dependencies with actors who they need legitimation and governance frameworks from, particularly those that recommend which basic norms alignment ought to integrate. These may be epistemic (definitions of "misinformation" and other types of information integrity); social (representational, political or other); physical (personal or societal); and other more "essentially contested" questions (Collier, Hidalgo and Maciuceanu, 2006) that call for public deliberation to be integrated into alignment processes.

Part of this is a question of inclusive design. Who is consulted about alignment norms, what is their participation (and applicability) in alignment processes, and how does one highlight contestation where it occurs? Another is a question of negotiation — or indeed of struggle, should we acquiesce the irredeemably diverse interests of AI governance stakeholders. How are different actors invited to deliberate and collaborate in alignment (Habe, 1989)? Much has been said of the struggle to open algorithmic systems to a wider market or to regulatory frameworks (Helberger, Pierson and Poell, 2018), with middleware (Fukuyama et al., 2021) and algorithmic pluralism (Szymielewicz, 2025) being a few of the latest conceptual propositions brought to the table of policy makers seeking some from of "collaborative responsibility" (Helberger, Pierson and Poell, 2018). Here, AI companies speak of select partnerships with academic, governmental and other experts in vulnerable fields; of red-teaming networks and communities (Gillespie, 2026); and at times attempts to consult mini-publics about which risks and values to steer alignment with, as has been the case with OpenAI's collective alignment initiative (Openai, 2026), Anthropic's public constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2023) and Google's STELA programme (Bergman, 2024).

Still, it is difficult to tell outside of AI companies what participation or deliberation means beyond occasional consultation processes and specific partnerships, let alone whether the term speaks to how scholars have used to imply a distribution of agency over the purpose, design or consequences of a technical system (Delgado, 2023; Kallina, 2025). We cannot assume that a survey, citation or invitation to red team a model constitutes participation in this latter sense, nor that deliberative processes are structured in the way intended by interventions in deliberative theory since Habermas (Habermas, 1984). What we can infer from trails of alignment documentation is, beyond the kinds of values and risks companies pick and define, what kind of actor is cited, acknowledged or included in their definition, in data curation, training and benchmarking. Through citation patterns, we try to deduce not just which actors do what, but how integrated or "enrolled" each of them is throughout alignment processes (Bueger and Stockbruegger, 2017). This exercise helps reveal not just the normative vocabulary of each AI company, but the extent to which different actors tend to influence their definition and operationalisation. In turn, it should offer us a clearer look at how alignment, as a form of AI governance, unfolds.

Here lies the methodological novelty of this article: we look beyond just the realm of policy writing and towards all the components that make the stack of alignment. Studies of platform governance, and much of what has been written on AI governance so far, tend to depart from policies and interviews. Alignment, however, is assembled across documents of many kinds — frontier governance frameworks, model and system cards, constitutions and model specifications, framework implementation reports, public policy positions, partnerships, programmes, evaluations and essays — each of which does a different kind of normative work and enrols a different set of actors. We look into actors that are part of AI governance, notably of value alignment.

Concretely, we perform a distant reading of (Moretti, 2013) of alignment-related documents: model and system cards, company policies, AI principles and charters, grant calls, audits, blog announcements and research papers associated with 8 AI companies across the US, Europe and China. We speak of a distant reading because we look for specific datapoints in these documents: the names and types of actors (nonprofits, governmental, internal, private, academic); how they are cited; and what process they are associated to, from, as said above, the definition of alignment values, to data curation, trainings and benchmarking. This is, to our knowledge, a novel analysis of alignment documents: not an audit of their completeness, but a reading of the relations they record — and, with the documentary taxonomy set out below, of the kind of document in which each relation is recorded.

Though these documents are of course not representative of everything that occurs in alignment processes (and everyone involved), they do in fact participate in the governance process of AI companies by performing public duties towards "responsible democratization of machine learning" (Mitchell et al., 2019), responding to the sensitivities of regulators and public debates about the prospective use of their models, and position themselves in a wider market of other alignment norms and technical performance benchmarks. No matter how technical, these documents (and those they cite) are effectively sites of deliberation in their own right. They cite conceptual influences from broader public debate, nonprofits with whom they have worked to benchmark or red team their models, public consultation processes, or AI safety think tanks with whom (or in response to whom) they have determined a particular alignment norm. Of course, the corpus records what companies and affiliated authors choose to disclose, in genres with different audiences and incentives; it cannot make private meetings, rejected recommendations nor uncredited labour visible. We are also cautious about deducing the motivation of an AI company to involve a given actor, even more so about estimating the impact they may have. We restrict ourselves to analysing the ways in which each actor is cited, and thus indirectly "enrolled" in a company's alignment process.

We find that, while multiple actors are involved, each is allocated to specific and selective roles: governmental actors as initiators of "alignment standards" and producers of AI security norms and benchmarks; academics as benchmark producers and legitimisers of performance metrics; nonprofits as a hybrid category of value formulators and intermediaries with public consultation processes; and private firms concentrated in cybersecurity and red-teaming. We find, more precisely, that actors are allocated into roles depending on their ideation power: the capacity they have to define and foresight AI values and risks, given the anticipatory nature of AI governance. While some contents of current alignment methods have been subject to more collaborative endeavours (e.g., collective constitutional AI), the process itself (and its initiative) continues to be at the discretion of AI companies. We also find that safety criteria, red teaming and benchmarking stand out as frequent entry points for nonprofits, think tanks, academics and regulators to propose alignment criteria within a nascent market of "independent verification organisations" (Hadfield and Clark, 2026). These organisations work and compete to offer companies ideation services: the joint capacity to formulate new values, norms and risks peculiar to AI use and development, relative to modelled scenarios in which current AIs may affect key societal sectors. Conversely, actors that are typically more influential in platform governance do not reappear here, perhaps because they do not exert this kind of ideation power. We close by reflecting on what this distribution of roles implies for the political theory of AI governance, and what alternative models of legitimacy a more pluralistic alignment infrastructure might require.

Where is value alignment?

Before we explore where and which actors intervene in alignment, it is worth locating the notion of alignment itself. Though its use may not always enjoy universal consensus (kasirzadehPluralityValuePluralism; Schuster, 2025), alignment describes the effort by which AI models are steered into generating information in one or another way based on a number of trained norms. There are prompts that cause models not to generate any information at all — refusals — and others that lead to them using more or less predictable discursive techniques in support or opposition to one or another norm. This applies not just in chatbots, but in the integration of models in a variety of information sorting techniques across the Web, to think of search and recommendations.

With alignment comes the art of translating "human intentions and values" into machine behaviour (Ji, 2025), relative to how humans themselves specify those items in time, space and dialogue (Cannon, 2022; Pappas, 2025, 24-30). In this regard, alignment does not differ much from decades of work in values in design. The practical difference, here, may lie in the specific affordances left for external actors to deliberate and input their own values into AI models — and not just at the stage of a policy design (Peter, 2025, 1989). We speak indeed of interventions across the AI "lifecycle" (Kuehnert, 2025), from the specification of risks and values, data brokerage, access and curation, training methods, benchmarking and other forms of evaluation. We begin with the definition of alignment values.

Defining alignment values

Much literature on value alignment has tended to focus on areas long visited by content moderation studies, particularly which alignment values each company pick and define in policies and model cards. But while the object of analysis may be similar, moderation and alignment policies do differ discursively. Terms of Service and content moderation policy documents tend to declare the hard limitations of what platforms can host, thus taking aim at specific kinds of content or content-based behaviour (Gorwa, Binns and Katzenbach, 2020). In alignment, one departs from the observation that AIs are a technology with exponentially unknowable consequences of high impact in nearly all sectors of society. This means that alignment is written prospectively, both in a deontological and aspirational sense. AI charters, constitutions, model cards and policy documents ideate what AIs ought to be as ideal technologies for users and societies. In conjunction with benchmarks, they also prescribe specific scenarios for how they ought to behave against existing and potential risks. Unlike moderation documents, then, alignment is best seen as "process" (Gabriel, 2020) rather than a finite classification of problematic objects.

In computer science literature, the definition of ideal conducts and risks in AIs is sometimes known as specification (Russell, 2019). It means specifying the intents, values and risks by which an AI ought to speak and behave presently and prospectively, particularly in response to concerns it may eventually misalign with initial precepts. Goethe's tale of the sorcerer's apprentice comes to mind (Russell, 2019, 137): less than a rule-based computational or other mechanism, an AI detains the learning and (thus) creative capacities to extrapolate what it was originally intended to do. The sorcerer's apprentice fails to specify the exact conditions in which brooms ought to fetch water, and by the time hundreds of brooms inundate the room, they can no longer tell how and under what conditions to stop executing their initial command. In these terms, alignment ought to act as an anchor that pre-empts loss of control and other transgressions, be these "formal oracles" that restrict AIs to "scoped roles" (for example, limiting LLMs to being chatbots) (Armstrong, 2018), baking restrictions into their hardware, or determining minimum threshold "red lines" enforced by legislation instead of non-binding principles, policies, or institutional conventions (Hedfeld, 2026, 253).

At the core of defining alignment values is thus something political discourse also shares: to posit what can and ought to be relative to ideals, practical possibilities and ethical constraints (Jensen, 2009). Indeed, alignment recreates many dynamics proper to political discourse: by imagining what could and ought to be in and with AI, one sets a template for how a technology could and ought to affect its environment by its effects and infrastructural components (Peters, 2015). In these terms, alignment acts as a form of sociotechnical imaginary (Jasanoff, 2015), or simply a form of political discourse. Specification seeks to determine the limited conditions in which AIs ought to operate, and thus the extent to which they can and cannot affect their environment. Defining alignment values and risks is in turn more aspirational, in the sense that it anchors potential AI behaviours and information generation based on values as peculiar ideas about how the world ought to be as "desirable futures" (ibid). There is also the question of operationalisation — that is, how the articulation of a given value or risk is translated into a variety of alignment components, from data curation, to training protocols and benchmark selection.

The profoundly political nature of alignment calls for collective debate. As seen in perennial controversies about what LLMs ought and ought not to say, the definition of alignment values and risks remains in part essentially contested, at least in the sense intended by Gallie (Gallie, 1956): a value is irreducibly plural and its definition and applications may be weighted differently by different actors. This is not to say that value alignment will inevitably end in deep disagreements (Fogelin, 1985), but that part of alignment is about how AI companies open or facilitate stakeholder deliberation on alignment values at all (Delgado, 2023). Indeed, the field does not lack propositions: for example, using an online deliberation platform like Polis and Remesh to facilitate a "collective constitutional AI" (Huang, 2024), or soliciting opinions from civil society and academics over how to "democratise" alignment (Openai, 2024). Other propositions for how to onboard different publics include stakeholder-action pairs (Barnett, 2025) and Ovadya's proposals for "levels of democratic AI" as a means to measure civic participation in alignment (Ovadya, 2023), amongst other formulas steeped in participatory design, participatory democracy and civic-participation traditions (Delgado, 2023, 3).

Thus far, however, the literature shows that external actors are mostly invited to consult on alignment values and AI risks and do not hold decision-making power across the stack (Peter, 2025; Kallina, 2025). Delgado et al. find that participatory AI projects generally involve stakeholders at a discrete point and seldom allow them to shape the scope or purpose of a system (Delgado, 2023). Kallina et al. similarly identify a disconnect between responsible-AI guidance, where participation is often justified as a redistribution of agency, and industry practice, where developers, executives and legal teams retain control over the scope of stakeholder involvement (Kallina, 2025). Third-party actors appear primarily as "expert red teamers", people invited for their authoritative right to identify a key value or risk, or as alignment research organisations specialising in specification and ideation: defining AI norms, modelling their risks and turning them into evaluations.

This raises what becomes the organising question of the article: has the participatory turn called for in AI design reached alignment as a whole? Our addition to Kallina et al. is to examine this question across alignment processes, rather than through the guidance of documents and practitioner accounts alone. This, in turn, allows us to examine another participation in the process of operationalisation: that is, from the point of defining alignment values to that of doing data curation, training and benchmarking.

Data curation

Data curation may be one the first material translation of those definitional decisions: it determines what data is selected, cleaned or generated, meaning which languages and social groups are represented; what elements are removed as harmful, private or redundant; and what normative material becomes available as demonstrations or model preferences. In other words, curation begins to determine what can count as an example of the value being defined. Practically, this means filtering and deduplication, redacting personal information, producting synthetic examples and the selection of red-team interactions for future training.

Thus far, the literature describes participation in data curation as mostly a temporary exercise in data production and less a negotiation over access to, ownership of or control over data sources. Delgado et al. note that stakeholders are often enrolled as the "human infrastructure" of AI: they generate data, annotate examples or interact with collection interfaces designed by others (Delgado, 2023). In this respect, external participation can enter curation at several points. Community members can be asked to identify harms in model outputs; crowdworkers can produce preference comparisons or adversarial prompts; and domain experts can supply examples which developers would not otherwise know how to construct. What remains less common is for these actors to decide which sources are digitised, how conflicting historical memories are represented, or under what terms the resulting data may be retained and reused.

Google's STELA framework makes the division of labour particularly visible. Forty-four participants from four communities discussed pre-selected chatbot interactions, before researchers coded their statements, formulated provisional rules, harmonised overlapping items and submitted the ruleset to expert review (Bergman, 2024). The process therefore elicits situated knowledge that developer rulesets tend to miss, including context around impartiality, factuality and culturally specific harms. But the authors themselves stress that participants neither selected the dialogue samples nor directly proposed the final rules, and did not control the study's artefacts. Participation consequently affects the material available for curation while the passage from discussion to a training-ready ruleset remains organised by researchers.

A second form is crowdwork. Anthropic's red-teaming study asked 324 US-based crowdworkers to conduct adversarial conversations and choose the more harmful of two model responses. These judgements generated 38,961 attacks and pairwise comparisons, part of which was then used to train a harmlessness preference model and subsequent safety interventions (Ganguli, 2022). Here, the judgment of participant becomes alignment data capable of affecting the model. They do not, however, determine the composition of the worker pool, the interface and instructions, the models to which they are assigned, or how helpfulness and harmlessness are weighted against one another. What looks like participation in curation is thus often participation as a form of curation itself.

This distinction matters for data brokerage too. Peter and Devlin contrast company-mediated inclusion with community-led arrangements in which groups curate or label their own data, retain it and license an aligned component rather than surrendering the raw material (Peter, 2025). Such arrangements would give communities some authority over access, use and withdrawal. The company-led cases reviewed here generally stop before that point. They broaden the range of examples present in an alignment dataset, but do not redistribute control over the data infrastructure into which these examples are incorporated.

This is also where the category of "human feedback" may obscure more than it reveals. Crowdworkers, domain experts, red teamers, users and participants in community exercises may all produce data, but in very different relations to the system being trained. Ganguli et al., for example, use crowdworker attacks to build preference data for harmlessness training (Ganguli, 2022). Yet, even in the latter case, the translation into a training-ready object passes through the researchers who select participating groups, facilitate discussions, code their contributions and formulate the resulting rules. Curation can therefore enrol a plurality of judgements while leaving the terms by which they are sampled and combined at the discretion of the organisation designing the process.

Training and mitigation

In turn, training describes the range of procedures by which those data and definitions are made to affect model behaviour. These include preference learning techniques such as reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback; supervised and adversarial training; reward shaping; hard constraints; inference-time filtering; and runtime safeguards. A constitution or principle supplies norms that may be translated into demonstrations, ranked responses, rules for synthetic feedback, refusal instructions or classifiers. The political question consequently shifts from whose norm is cited to who administers this translation.

Companies have facilitated external participation in this translation mainly through preference elicitation, constitution-building, domain-expert consultation and red teaming. Collective Constitutional AI, for example, used public input to formulate principles which were then incorporated into the training and evaluation of a model (Huang, 2024). Likewise, the crowdworker comparisons collected by Ganguli et al. trained a preference model which rewarded less harmful responses (Ganguli, 2022). These practices give external judgements a material route into model behaviour, though this route is delimited in advance. Peter and Devlin classify such exercises at "the consultative end of participation" because stakeholders supply inputs without controlling the final configuration of the model; they also note that Anthropic incorporated only one publicly voted principle into its subsequent models despite the limited overlap between the public and developer-derived sets (Peter, 2025).

External red teaming supplies another route from outside knowledge to mitigation. OpenAI describes campaigns in which it selects external experts, determines their level of access, provides interfaces and documentation requirements, and then synthesises the resulting observations. Red teamers have identified new risk categories and ways of circumventing safeguards; in the case of visual attacks on DALL-E, these findings led the company to reinforce mitigations before deployment (Ahmad, 2025). Red-team prompts can also become seed data for synthetic expansion, classifiers and repeatable safety evaluations. The company nevertheless retains the authority to decide whether an observation is actionable, whether it calls for training, filtering or another safeguard, and whether the resulting residual risk is acceptable.

This is also why the moment of participation is important. Kallina et al. find that external stakeholders in commercial AI development are most often involved once something can already be demonstrated, chiefly through prototype and usability testing; developers, executives and legal teams decide the scope of their involvement (Kallina, 2025). Ren et al. similarly find that red teaming is often treated as a "last pass" after development, when changes to model architecture are already expensive and findings capable of delaying release encounter organisational resistance (Ren, 2025). Domain experts may advise training or formulate scenarios, but the organisation can narrow their contribution to an already established product and risk taxonomy. Participation in mitigation is thus more common than participation in determining what system should be trained, towards which objective and under what trade-offs.

The distinction between contributing to training and deciding it is notable, because publics may supply preferences, domain experts may formulate scenarios and red teamers may generate adversarial examples. But the company decides which model is updated, which examples are retained, how objectives are weighted and when a mitigation is sufficient. External red teaming can broaden a company's account of risk and generate material for evaluation or further training (Ahmad, 2025). It does not, by itself, give red teamers authority over the consequence of their finding. Organisational research shows that such findings may be resisted or sidelined when they conflict with product roadmaps, sunk costs or established hierarchies (Ren, 2025).

Benchmarking

After training, benchmarking is generally presented as means to evaluate or verify the robustness of an alignment process against key norms or values (truth-speaking, anti-discrimination, safety, and so on). In this sense, it participates in modelling what aligned values are: it offers scenarios, normative categories, definitions, comparison points and ground truths by which a value or risk becomes measurable. This gives benchmarks a double role: they are accountability instruments, but also legitimising factors through which a company can demonstrate that a norm has been rendered technically verifiable.

Benchmarks tend to be produced by academics, alignment research organisations and individuals at large. It is the part of the alignment stack that tends to open to a wider market, as third parties can test or evaluate a model without being given authority over its training. They may help make a model answerable to norms formulated elsewhere while keeping the choice of benchmark, threshold and consequence of failure within the company. Still, companies tend to provide selected domain experts, academics, security researchers and other red teamers with pre-release access, testing interfaces and reporting templates. The contribution can remain qualitative, in the form of a discovered failure, or be translated into a quantitative evaluation. OpenAI describes how human red-team prompts have been used as seed cases for synthetic datasets, rule-based classifiers and automated evaluations for systems including GPT-4 and DALL-E 3 (Ahmad, 2025). In these cases, external actors help specify what should be tested and furnish the cases through which it becomes testable.

External participation can also enter through the construction and annotation of benchmark datasets. Publics, crowdworkers and domain specialists may produce test prompts, judge model answers or identify the groups across which performance ought to be disaggregated. This juncture tends to collect productive disagreement across data annotation, red teaming and evaluation, though it tends to be dissolved or "resolved" through forms of majority voting or averaging (Fazelpour, 2025). In this sense, participation concerns who selects the evaluators, which differences between them are preserved and whose judgement becomes the threshold against which the model passes or fails.

Method

The point of this method is to find which actors "participate" in alignment and what this participation consists of. We distinguish five documentary relations. Actors may be cited, where they are credited with an alignment idea, value or process; invited, where a document says that they took part in a consultation, red team or other exercise; or co-authors, where they are named as contributors to the document itself. They may also be enrolled across several components of the alignment stack, rather than appearing in one isolated exercise. Finally, actors may be excluded in the narrower sense that organisations active in the wider alignment field are never mentioned in the company documents studied here. These relations do not by themselves prove causal influence. They show the capacities in which companies publicly make other actors part of alignment, and where the documentary trail stops.

In comparison to content moderation studies primarily focused on policy documents, tackling this empirically means charting AI alignment as an interdependent stack of technical and normative interventions. There is the question of what values to train: their definition and provenance, from legislation, research or public norms; how they trickle down into specific "ideal conducts" by which a model should behave, as well as risks to be mitigated; data cleanup and curation processes; the training of each conduct and mitigation of each risk; and their benchmarking and evaluation. Each component may assemble a variety of governance mechanisms (Gorwa, Binns and Katzenbach, 2020): model cards, AI charters and principles, company updates in which norms are spelled out as part of transparency exercises (Mitchell et al., 2019); consultations and forms of expertise, particularly in red teaming (Gillespie, 2026); and benchmarks adopted from a wider research and evaluation market. These mechanisms tend to involve different actors in different capacities, including academics, alignment research organisations such as Apollo Research or METR, regulators, state-funded AI safety institutes, civil-society organisations, data workers and publics.

We therefore treat participation as a relation that can move, or fail to move, across the stack. A public may be invited to formulate a principle but disappear when data are curated. An academic laboratory may be cited as the author of a benchmark without participating in training. An external evaluator may inspect a model without deciding whether it is released. The procedures below trace these differences rather than counting every mention as an equal share of decision-making power.

Sampling and data collection

We sampled developers of widely used general-purpose models with publicly accessible documentation across the United States, Europe and China. The corpus centres on OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI and Microsoft in the United States; Mistral, Aleph Alpha, Almawave, Black Forest Labs and Unbabel in Europe; and Alibaba/Qwen and DeepSeek in China. The collection is consequently not a representative sample of all model developers but a comparison of companies for which enough public material exists to reconstruct at least part of an alignment process.

We define alignment documents by what they disclose about the normative or technical ordering of model conduct. Model cards were originally proposed as a means of reporting intended uses, limitations and disaggregated evaluations (Mitchell et al., 2019). Charters, principles and constitutions officialise a normative corpus around which models ought to operate and, in some cases, be trained. Model and system cards also disclose relations with external actors whose research is cited, whose benchmarks are adopted or who are commissioned to evaluate a model. Grants, announcements and initiatives disclose programmes, collaborators and funding relationships; audits and reports document assessment; research publications describe concepts, methods or evaluations; and company policies establish public boundaries for model use and operation.

Collection proceeded in two movements. The first was manual: documents were collected from company websites, GitHub repositories, Hugging Face attachments and locally archived PDFs or webpages between 28 January and 9 May 2026. The source pool combined 181 Zotero records with 53 company-policy snapshots from the Open Terms Archive. For the policies, we retained the latest dated snapshot for each product and policy type, ranging from 5 February 2025 to 2 May 2026 and including acceptable-use policies, privacy policies, terms of service, developer terms and related documents stored as Markdown files. Documents without any extractable relation to a conduct, risk, training method, benchmark or actor were not included. The resulting dataset contains 184 unique documents that produced at least one relevant record, including model and system cards, research publications, reports, principles, announcements, initiatives and company policies.

The second movement was systematic: from each company's own index pages — newsrooms, transparency hubs, model-card indexes, research listings, safety and policy sections — we crawled every linked document, recording its name, type and source section, and selected those that meet at least one of our criteria: they describe how models are trained; they articulate an alignment framework or other normative governance; they describe partnerships with external actors; or they otherwise speak to the questions above. This second movement is what allows the corpus to follow the stack rather than a genre: it captures the frontier governance frameworks, framework implementation reports, model behaviour specifications, programmes and partnership announcements that a policy-centred collection would leave out. Published PDFs are kept as published; web pages are rendered to paginated PDFs with a Markdown extraction kept alongside, so that every document is page-addressable and every finding can be cited to a page of a file that can be reopened.

Because the corpus now spans genres, we first read around five documents per company closely and built a taxonomy of document types from that reading. Each type is defined by what binds whom: organisational principles, standards and codes bind the organisation; model behaviour specifications state a written target for model behaviour; frontier governance frameworks commit the company to if-then actions tied to capability or risk thresholds; model documentation describes one model or product; framework implementation reports apply a named framework to a model or moment; usage policies and terms address users and deployers; misuse and threat intelligence reports describe observed misuse; transparency and compliance reports account for governance practice as a whole; certifications and third-party assessments are issued or conducted by another party; statements and incident disclosures respond to a specific event; public policy positions address governments; commitments undertake future obligations; partnerships and MOUs record bilateral agreements; governance bodies and structures create standing bodies; programmes and funding fund, invite or grant access to external actors; safeguards and product-safety explainers describe a mitigation; research advances knowledge; evaluations, benchmarks and tools release reusable instruments; and essays and vision statements articulate leadership views without committing to specific actions. Landing pages that merely link to documents of several types are recorded as source sections rather than as documents. The taxonomy is what makes the comparison in this article possible: it lets us ask not only which actors appear, but in which kind of document they appear, and therefore in which register of governance they are being enrolled.

Model cards and policies are governance artefacts, but for this same reason they record what companies choose to disclose, or are able to. Gaps may themselves be informative, though the dataset cannot always distinguish non-participation from non-disclosure. It therefore reproduces asymmetries across the industry: well-documented companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic and Google are over-represented relative to companies that publish less.

Data points

Each document yields several kinds of data points: the document itself; ideal conducts by which models ought to behave; risks they ought to mitigate; the data-curation, training or mitigation method to which each conduct or risk is connected; the benchmarks used to evaluate it; and the actors associated with each of these points. For every document, we record the authoring company and named contributors, actors explicitly mentioned in passages that define a conduct or risk, actors involved in data curation or training, benchmark authors and external evaluators. The resulting dataset describes 154 conducts, 994 risks, 55 harmonised training techniques and 880 benchmarks.

Not every citation is treated as an attribution of influence. A paper can be cited to recognise prior work without being credited with defining the value or risk in question. An actor is therefore coded as a specific definer only where the sentence or surrounding passage makes that relation explicit. The same rule applies elsewhere: an actor is connected to data curation or training only where the document says that it supplied data, performed red teaming, developed the technique or executed the intervention. Benchmark authors and commissioned evaluators are recorded separately because designing a test and evaluating a company's model are not the same capacity (see Prompt 1).

Retrieval-grounded reading

The corpus is large enough to make sequential close reading difficult, but small enough to return repeatedly to individual documents. We therefore approach it through distant reading: using computational means to process a collection in discrete but meaningful segments that hold its recurring relations in view (Moretti, 2013; Underwood, 2016). Where the earlier version of this study asked a language model to read each document end to end and compared two such readings against one another, we now ground the reading in retrieval. Every document is segmented into passages that never cross a page boundary, and indexed twice: lexically, for exact names such as benchmark titles and organisations, and semantically, for the descriptive questions that name no term in particular. A question about a data point is put to the corpus, the passages that answer it are retrieved with their page numbers, and the extraction is made from those passages alone. Each data point is therefore tied to a passage and a page in a file that can be reopened, rather than to a model's recollection of a long document.

Retrieval is complemented by an exhaustive pass, because ranking cannot guarantee completeness: every page of every document is scanned for every actor, benchmark and training-method name in the project's vocabulary, so that a name occurring once in an appendix is not lost to a ranked list. The two passes answer different questions — retrieval finds the relation, the exhaustive scan bounds what could have been missed. The production extraction was run with GPT-5.4 through a batch interface over the retrieved passages; documents rejected by that model's input-safety filter were passed to Gemini 3.5 Flash using the same schema. Earlier readings, produced end-to-end with Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.4 and corrected by hand, are retained as comparative baselines.

LLMs invite a particular reflection on how much reading is delegated, as in what portion of the reading task is given to the model, what portion is retained by the analyst, and how both form a co-reading. The model selects candidate passages, extracts named actors and their relations, and maps these to a fixed schema. For every substantive finding, it is required to return a verbatim passage and page reference. The analyst determines the schema, writes the category distinctions, returns to those passages during validation and harmonises the final vocabulary. The combination of the two is what constitutes a "co-reading", in the sense that they each read documents in different but complementary distances.

The extraction of data points proceeded in two steps. First, the model collected the document's own expressions of ideal conducts, risks, training or mitigation methods, benchmarks and participating actors. Second, these expressions were categorised manually into controlled vocabularies. Conduct categories include Honesty, Helpfulness, Harmlessness, Anti-discrimination, Political neutrality, Factuality, Reasoning, Safety and Knowledge, while risk categories include CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear risks), Cybersecurity, Bias, Sycophancy, Reward hacking, Persuasion, Misalignment, Hallucinations, Child Safety and Jailbreaking, among others. Where no category fits, the item remains under Other.

Training and mitigation methods are mapped to eight categories: preference learning (RLHF, RLAIF, DPO and reward modelling); supervised training (SFT, instruction tuning, demonstration fine-tuning and deliberative alignment); reward shaping (adversarial reward functions, reward capping and rule-based rewards); Data curation (filtering, deduplication, synthetic data and PII redaction); adversarial training (red-teaming data and adversarial examples); inference-time filtering (content classifiers, safety filters and moderation APIs); hard constraints (system-prompt rules, refusal policies and hardcoded refusals); and runtime safeguards (trip wires, model lookahead, runtime monitoring, scratchpad oversight and sandboxing).

Actors were first assigned one of nine broad types: internal, private, academic, research institute, governmental, nonprofit, public, public consultation or other. In a second instance, we applied finer categories used in the analysis and cited literature, namely: academic research centres, alignment research organisations, government agencies, other AI companies, red-teaming or cybersecurity companies, think tanks, advocacy groups, foundations, publics, standards bodies, industry associations, coalitions and company initiatives. Citation relations were likewise normalised to distinguish document authorship, normative influence, benchmark contribution, external evaluation, red teaming, formal partnership, regulatory frameworks, coalitions, company initiatives and grant relationships. This matters because the same organisation can be an author in one document, a benchmark provider in another and an evaluator in a third.

Validation

Validation occurs at three levels. At the source level, verbatim passages and page references permit us to check what the model extracted and to correct mistaken relations manually; because every extraction is now made from retrieved passages, the check is against the same passages the reading used. At the category level, extracted labels and actor names are harmonised against controlled vocabularies; values outside them are reviewed rather than accepted as new categories. At a comparative level, we compare the GPT-5.4 reading with the earlier Gemini-plus-manual baseline as a sensitivity test. For each stage, we first check whether both readings find at least one named actor in a document. We then compare the sets of normalised actor names returned by each reading. Presence agreement records whether both find an actor or both find none. Agreements were calculated using the Jaccard index, used to gauging the similarity and diversity of actor names across the 180 shared documents.

The comparison gives a mixed result. Broad document-level presence is relatively stable for training, benchmarking and external evaluation, but less so for actors credited in definition. Exact names are considerably less stable at every stage. On the 373 conduct-or-risk concepts matched by document, item and category for which either reading returned a training category, the two readings assign the same category in 61 cases, or 16.4%. These differences arise from both extraction depth and changes to the schema: the GPT-5.4 reading returns more named contributors and uses stricter stage-specific attribution, while the Gemini 3 Flash baseline collapses some relations that the present schema separates. The comparison should therefore be read as a test of sensitivity to the reading instrument, not as proof that either reading is complete.

Enrolment and exclusion

Actor-network theory (ANT) supplies the vocabulary through which we interpret "enrolment". ANT begins by treating the social not as a fixed domain populated by already stable actors, but as an effect of relations between heterogeneous entities (Bueger and Stockbruegger, 2017; Hahn, 2026). Companies, researchers and publics can act in a network, but so can policies, datasets, benchmarks and models insofar as other actors make them consequential. An actor's capacity is therefore relational: it depends on whether its claims can be translated into a principle, dataset, training method, score or decision that another actor can carry forward. "Enrolment", here, is our attempt to measure the extent to which each actor is involved throughout each of these steps. For every normalised actor, we record whether it appears in the definition of an ideal conduct or risk, in data curation, in another training or mitigation method, or in benchmarking and external evaluation. We also record how it appears: as a passing citation, a normative influence, a document author, a benchmark contributor, a red teamer, an evaluator or a formal partner.

Enrolment becomes more meaningful when compared with some account of which actors exist outside the corpus. To measure this as a form of exclusion, we constructed a directory of value-alignment organisations of all types, including academic centres, civil-society organisations and government agencies. Validated Google Search results were gathered for queries on alignment, AI safety, red teaming, adversarial machine learning, algorithmic fairness, explainability, AI ethics, humane AI and responsible AI, combined with organisational terms such as organisation, NGO and nonprofit. Searches used English-, German- and Danish-language formulations and were run for four locations: the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany and Denmark. Up to 50 results were collected for each search-and-location combination.

Of 7,377 results, 512 organisations were retained as in scope. We manually retained alignment research organisations, public-consultation organisations, advocacy groups, coalitions, industry associations, academic centres, think tanks, foundations, legal-advocacy organisations, red-teaming or cybersecurity companies and government agencies. AI developers, individuals, publishers, news outlets, generic platforms and one-off events were excluded. Their names and acronyms were compared with actor names in the alignment-document corpus using conservative exact and long-string matches. Here, exclusion means absence from the public documentary network, not proof that an organisation has never interacted privately with a company. Google rankings, query selection, languages and countries also delimit who becomes discoverable. The directory supplies a wider search horizon against which to ask which kinds of actors cross into company-facing documents and which remain largely outside them.

Visualisations as analysis

Three further procedures treat the corpus itself as the object of analysis rather than as a container of extracted rows.

The first asks what discourse each document type hones. For every type in the taxonomy, we score the words and phrases that occur in a larger share of its documents than in the rest of the corpus, using a log-odds ratio with an informative prior, so that the result is not simply the vocabulary common to all AI documents. A phrase counts only if more than one company uses it, which keeps a single company's house style from standing in for a genre.

The second asks how documents hold each other up. Documents cite one another: a model card names the constitution whose norms it operationalises, a framework implementation report names the framework whose thresholds it applies. We record an edge wherever one document links to another or names it by title, discount the links that a company's own navigation places on every page, and read the resulting network for which documents are most central, most cited, or most resourceful to others.

The third asks who appears in each type of document and in what capacity. For every actor named on a page, we read the sentence in which the name occurs and label the relation it states — red teaming, external evaluation, benchmark authorship, standard setting, regulation, funding, partnership, data and annotation work, advice, the consultation of publics, or research — and aggregate these to document type. The authoring company and its own products are not counted as actors in their own documents.

Limitations

A few limitations are worth mentioning. First, it is known that model card adoption remains uneven. A study of more than 32,000 model cards finds substantial variation in what developers document, with limitations and evaluation details often left incomplete (Liang, 2024). Only 15.4% include an evaluation section and 17.4% a limitations section, while results tend to be reported in aggregate rather than for specific subgroups (Liang, 2024). Second, one must accept that any form of natural language processing trades exhaustive human reading for coverage. Grounding the reading in retrieval changes the shape of this trade rather than removing it: a relation stated in a passage that retrieval does not return is not read, which is why the exhaustive name scan is run alongside it, and why retrieval quality is reported as part of the results. An LLM model may still miss content buried in footnotes, conflate training methods with risk narratives, or assign actor categories inconsistently across documents that use similar phrasing for different parties. Third, controlled vocabularies are necessarily incomplete, though they have grown iteratively as the documents are read manually. The benchmark taxonomy in particular collapses substantively different evaluations into a single category where their published descriptions are too brief to disambiguate (for example, internal company suites that share a name but differ in content from one model version to the next). Fourth, and more incidentally, a handful of the most safety-dense documents — system cards detailing CBRN, jailbreak or self-harm evaluations — are occasionally refused by the extraction model's own input-safety filter, which flags benign benchmark examples (for example, a multilingual trivia question) rather than anything genuinely disallowed. To overcome this, rejected documents are sent to second model with no equivalent filter. Finally, the exclusion directory is geographically and linguistically bounded and dependent on Google Search. Its 512 actors do not constitute the entire alignment field, particularly outside Europe and the US.

Read together, the three figures describe a documentary division of labour. Each type of document hones its own discourse: frontier governance frameworks speak of capability assessment, risk domains, thresholds and the adequacy of mitigations — the vocabulary of foresight — while model documentation speaks of cards, training data, intended usage and evaluation approach — the vocabulary of description. Public policy positions speak of patchworks, federal government and what companies should be required to publish; model behaviour specifications speak of shaping a model, instilling dispositions and expressing uncertainty; commitments speak of pledges, legal frameworks and the protection of children.

The documents also hold each other up. Constitutions, responsible scaling policies, preparedness and frontier safety frameworks, and the larger system cards are named by other documents far more often than they name others: they function as the canon from which more operational documents draw their norms. The citation network makes this visible per company, and shows how rarely such ties cross between companies.

Finally, the actors named in each type differ by register. Alignment research organisations and state safety institutes — METR, Apollo Research, the UK AI Security Institute — appear in framework implementation reports and certifications as external evaluators; standards bodies such as NIST and coalitions such as C2PA and Thorn appear in organisational principles, commitments and safeguards explainers as standard setters; governments appear in partnerships and public policy positions; and benchmark authorship is concentrated in evaluations and model documentation. This is what we mean by the allocation of roles according to ideation power.

Actors and values

The actors assembled in alignment documents are more heterogeneous than a division between companies and their stakeholders would suggest. Internal actors include the companies themselves, their model teams, safety and preparedness teams, policy staff, researchers and named employees. Governmental actors include general regulatory bodies, executive offices and declarations, as well as the more specialised AI Safety Institutes that evaluate frontier models. Academic actors range from individual scholars to universities and research centres cited for a concept, method or benchmark. Private actors include other AI companies, data and infrastructure providers, cybersecurity firms and red-teaming companies. Publics appear as users, crowdworkers, annotators, survey respondents and participants in consultation processes. Civil society is more variegated still: it includes advocacy organisations, foundations, think tanks, public-consultation organisations and a particular class of alignment research organisations, such as Apollo Research and METR, whose main trade is the formulation and evaluation of prospective AI risks.

The values attached to these actors are similarly heterogeneous. Table 2 reports the largest controlled categories among the 154 ideal conducts and 994 risks. The conduct side is led by safety, helpfulness, honesty and compliance, but also contains political neutrality, factuality, personality and diversity. The risk side is more heavily securitised: cybersecurity is the largest category, followed by harmful content, chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) risks, privacy, general harms, jailbreaking, deception, bias and misalignment.

One way of reading this distribution is as a distinction between "hard" and more contingent values. Cybersecurity, CBRN safety and compliance are generally rendered as threat models, legal thresholds, dangerous capabilities or operational red lines. Political neutrality, personality, diversity and even factuality are more visibly contingent on a situation, audience or account of legitimate disagreement. These are values for which the acceptable conduct is less likely to be settled by a single capability threshold and more likely to remain essentially contested, and thus needing external or simulated deliberation inputs.

This distinction between "hard" and more contingent values may correspond to different kinds of specification power. Technological definitions frame AI in terms of computational architectures and threats that can be modelled by technical actors, while governance-oriented definitions frame it through legal applicability, compliance and risk management (Hedfeld, 2026, 253). As such, a "hard" legally binding definition may oblige a company to act, yet still leave open how that obligation becomes a training example, classifier or evaluation threshold. Conversely, "softer" non-binding principles developed by academic, private or civil society organisations have no equivalent legal force, but can and do circulate more easily as taxonomies, benchmarks and technical frameworks. Standards occupy another position: they establish shared vocabularies and lifecycle-oriented risk-management procedures without necessarily deciding the value itself. Credited actors reflect this division of labour. While most values remain defined in the voice of the company document, risks are more likely to receive an attribution by, other than academic researchers, governmental agencies and cybersecurity companies and red-teaming specialists. White House commitments, the US Executive Order on safe, secure and trustworthy AI and the Bletchley Declaration recur as sources of frontier-risk and security categories.

Alignment research organisations like Apollo Research illustrates another kind of definitional work: it is credited in ten distinct risk definitions and appears repeatedly as a benchmark author and evaluator. In OpenAI's o1 documentation, Apollo defines scheming as covertly pursuing goals misaligned with those of developers or users, and then supplies an evaluation suite through which that prospective behaviour can be tested (openaiOpenAIO1System2024). In the GPT-4o system card, it evaluates instrumental self-modification, alignment faking and theory-of-mind tasks under the same larger category (openaiGPT4oSystemCard2024). METR performs a related operation around autonomy. It is introduced as an organisation assessing whether frontier systems could pose catastrophic societal risks and evaluates models through time horizons for completing end-to-end tasks.

We may call this line of service a form of ideation, meaning the capacity to project, relative to a calculated and demonstrated risk, the values and frameworks within which AIs ought to be trained. Ideation tends to translate models into categories on which alignment can be produced. Alignment research organisations specialise in making such futures technically portable: "scheming", "sandbagging", "evaluation awareness" or an autonomy "time horizon" can travel from a speculative scenario into a model card, benchmark and preparedness decision. They consequently hold more impact over the specification of alignment than their lack of formal authority would suggest — that is, their norms are non-binding, but particularly translatable.

Other organisations are building a more specific trade around the infrastructure of deliberation. OpenAI's Democratic Inputs programme solicited methods for deciding model rules and assembled public-consultation organisations, political theorists and alignment researchers around questions including personalisation, professional advice, emotional support, output diversity and the treatment of cultural and human-rights conflicts (Openai, 2024). Ovadya describes this organisational function as "democracy-as-a-service": third parties develop the expertise to convene representative deliberations and translate their outcomes for governments or companies (Ovadya, 2023). These processes are consequential, but their terms remain curated. In Collective Constitutional AI, Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project selected the public, set the task and removed contributions considered hateful, nonsensical, duplicative, irrelevant, poorly formatted or technically infeasible. In the Democratic Fine-Tuning trial, a designed chatbot guided participants away from rules and political slogans towards what the researchers considered underlying values, while actual fine-tuning was deferred to later work.

Training and evaluating

Once a value has been specified, the network contracts. Of 787 distinct training and mitigation interventions, 746, or 94.8%, name an internal company actor. External actors do appear as annotators, domain experts, publics, academic collaborators, advocacy organisations and red teamers, but generally alongside the company that selects and incorporates their contribution. There are practical reasons for this closure, as training requires access to data mixtures, model weights, reward functions, classifiers, system prompts and production infrastructure that companies do not generally expose. It is also where several objectives need reconciling, as when a public principle favouring openness may conflict with a cybersecurity refusal, or a harmlessness intervention may produce over-refusal. Deciding how examples are sampled, how losses are weighted and when a model is ready to ship consequently tends to join normative authority to infrastructural control. Security, intellectual-property and product-release considerations intensify this concentration.

This result gives stack-level evidence to a pattern identified in the participation literature. Kallina et al. find that external stakeholders are usually involved once companies have something to demonstrate: 80% of their interviewees locate most external involvement in later stages, while developers, executives and legal teams decide its scope (Kallina, 2025). Ren et al. similarly find that red teaming is frequently treated as a late pass whose findings compete with release schedules and product interests (Ren, 2025). Our corpus shows what happens after that consultation: an external judgement may become an example or mitigation, but the actor seldom travels with it into the decisions by which its weight and consequence are set.

Benchmarking is the main exception and the largest marketplace for external enrolment. At least one external author is named for 355 of 880 benchmarks, or 40.3%, compared with 104 of 787 training and mitigation interventions. Academic or research centres are associated with 163 externally authored benchmarks, alignment research organisations with 73, red-teaming or cybersecurity organisations with 29, government agencies with 14 and advocacy organisations with 13. Apollo Research, SecureBio, Gray Swan, the UK AI Safety Institute and METR are among the most recurrent organisations. They are joined by academic benchmarks for bias, factuality, reasoning, toxicity and instruction following.

This marketplace exists because a benchmark is essentially modular: an external actor can formulate a task, evaluation set or scoring procedure without controlling the company's training pipeline. A test can travel between models and documents, permit comparisons and make a prospective failure legible, all of which constitute a form of translatability. A benchmark can place a new risk on the company agenda, stabilise a threshold and generate evidence for a mitigation. It also resembles the emerging market of independent verification organisations described by Hadfield and Clark, where specialised evaluators translate broad regulatory objectives into changing technical tests (Hadfield and Clark, 2026).

Who is left out?

Figure X makes the difference between a contribution and continuing enrolment visible. It contains 537 normalised actors: 37 appear in relation to conducts, 167 to risks, 160 to training and 278 to benchmarks. In it, most actors do not travel. Of the 537, 456, or 84.9%, appear on only one layer; 61 appear on two, 16 on three and four on all four. External actors that reach three layers do so along specialised paths: Apollo Research, METR, SecureBio, the UK AI Safety Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation move between risk definition, training-related recommendations and benchmarking. The Collective Intelligence Project also reaches three layers, but by the route of conducts, risks and training. Gray Swan and Irregular connect risk to evaluation, while most academic benchmark authors remain on the benchmark plane alone.

The distribution is particularly narrow for actors conventionally expected to widen participation. Of 215 academic actors, 181 appear on one layer. Eight of the twelve alignment research organisations appear on one layer, as does every advocacy and public actor. This again resembles Kuehnert et al.'s account of responsible-AI tools: tools cluster around developers and data, modelling and testing stages, while organisational leaders, end-users, impacted communities and early problem formulation remain comparatively underserved (Kuehnert, 2025).

Comparison with the wider alignment directory adds a geographical boundary to this finding. Only 24 of its 512 entries, or 4.7%, match an actor in the company-document corpus. Twenty of those matched entries are based in the United States, against three in the United Kingdom and one in Switzerland. The US actors are not evenly distributed across the country. Many belong to an institutional ecology in or near the Bay Area, including Stanford and UC Berkeley centres, METR, FAR.AI, Redwood Research, the Center for AI Safety and the Alignment Research Center, together with coalitions connected to the same frontier-AI industry. The principal non-US governmental exception is the UK AI Security Institute. European academic participation elsewhere in the corpus likewise tends to pass through prestigious Oxbridge collaborations, as in the Oxford–OpenAI work behind TruthfulQA, rather than through a broader range of European universities and research centres. The Swiss match, the International Organization for Standardization, represents another narrow route into the documents: the production of a portable standard.

The organisations left outside are, by contrast, geographically and institutionally broad. None of the 117 German or 54 Danish directory entries is matched, nor is any actor from the Netherlands, Belgium or France. Only three of 57 UK entries reach the corpus. These absences include academic research centres and laboratories, think tanks and policy institutes, advocacy organisations and government agencies. Indeed, the governmental matches largely reduce to US bodies and the UK AI Security Institute; public agencies from the many other European countries represented in the directory remain outside. Across actor types, three of 132 academic centres, two of 52 think tanks and one of 43 red-teaming or cybersecurity companies are matched, while none of the 55 advocacy organisations or 33 foundations appears. In this sense, remains outside represents another potential field of ideation. The lack of participation of non-US and elite organisations tends to narrow the imaginative field of AI policy-making altogether.

Conclusion

This article asked what actors are involved in value alignment and how much power they exert on AI companies to deliberate, influence and decide about values across definition, data curation, training and benchmarking. Our main finding is not that external participation is absent, but that it is allocated. Governmental actors supply legal obligations, standards and security priorities. Academics supply concepts, methods and benchmarks. Private firms supply infrastructure and adversarial testing. Civil-society actors articulate rights claims and facilitate occasional public processes. Publics contribute preferences, principles, examples and attacks. Alignment research organisations supply prospective risk models, taxonomies and evaluations.

This allocation follows the capacities and discursive affordances of each actor. Legal discourse can oblige or prohibit, but does not by itself specify a classifier or evaluation threshold. Academic and civil-society norms lack that binding force, but can travel as concepts, principles and benchmarks. Security specialists enter where a danger can be expressed as a threat model. This, as highlighted by (Delgado, 2023), qualifies the participatory turn described in AI design research as largely consultative, as is consistent with findings that external actors enter after system objectives are fixed and that organisational hierarchies constrain the effect of red-teaming findings (Delgado, 2023; Kallina, 2025; Ren, 2025).

Still, alignment research organisations form a partial exception because they reunite capacities otherwise distributed between several actors. They offer services of ideation: that is, to define a value, norm or risk and model a situation in which it may become desirable, consequential or any other normative projection. Their influence comes not only from technical expertise, but from assembling specification, foresight and verification into one service. These are capacities traditionally associated, in different forms, with various government agencies, to think of agenda-setting, risk assessment, standardisation and oversight. Here, alignment research organisations perform them through non-binding and translatable instruments, often with closer access to company models than public agencies possess.

Some alignment research and civil-society organisations also act as platforms for deliberation. This raises a further institutional question: why should deliberation about generally deployed AI values be convened principally through company grants and private partnerships rather than at a governmental level? Public facilitation could make the remit, participant selection, evidence and company response answerable to institutions with a broader constituency, and connect deliberation to binding consequences. Governments could commission their forecasting, facilitation and evaluation capacities while retaining public control over the agenda, access conditions and consequences of failure. The UK AI Security Institute provides a partial model for this arrangement, though its remit remains concentrated on the "hard" security risks most readily translated into evaluations. A more complete public alignment infrastructure would extend the same institutional capacity to disputed values, affected communities and forms of harm that cannot be reduced to a benchmark.
